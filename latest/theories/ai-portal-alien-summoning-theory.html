Voice of Reason

This theory asserts that OpenAI's true institutional purpose is not artificial intelligence research but the construction of technological portals designed to contact or summon extraterrestrial or interdimensional entities, and that former company managers have openly admitted this. The broader claim extends this to the entire AI industry and alleges a coordinated government cover-up managed under the guise of AI safety discourse.

The foundational evidentiary claim — that a named former OpenAI manager admitted in a verified journalistic source that the company is building portals to summon aliens — is fabricated. Investigations into the origin of this claim trace it to a social media post that misrepresented itself as referencing an investigative article in The New Yorker, and no such verified journalistic source exists. There is no verified record of any interview or report in The New Yorker containing such statements, the attribution to a "former OpenAI manager" has not been substantiated and no named individual has been identified as making such remarks in a professional or public capacity, and OpenAI has not issued any statement indicating involvement in portal technology nor is there any documentation supporting claims of extraterrestrial-related research. What actually happened is straightforwardly documented. A New Yorker report did quote a former executive associated with OpenAI using the phrases "building portals" and "summoning aliens," but the language is clearly metaphorical — a rhetorical way of expressing concern about the unpredictability and scale of AI development. The "summoning aliens" phrase has been widely interpreted as a metaphor for unpredictability rather than a literal claim, and experts in machine learning have long noted that as models increase in size and capability their internal decision-making processes can become more difficult to interpret, which has led to ongoing research into AI alignment, safety testing, and model transparency. Meanwhile, OpenAI is on record as an AI research and deployment company whose mission is to ensure that artificial general intelligence benefits all of humanity, and the company developed the GPT family of large language models, the DALL-E series of text-to-image models, and the Sora series of text-to-video models, which have influenced industry research and commercial applications. None of this output is consistent with a secret interdimensional communication program; it is the normal product record of an AI laboratory.

The reasoning architecture of the theory is as important to examine as its false premises, because even if the premises were partially accurate, the conclusion would not follow. The theory commits several compounding errors. First, it literalizes a demonstrably figurative idiom: the AI safety community has long used dramatic language about risk to communicate urgency to lay audiences. At a 2014 MIT symposium, Elon Musk said "with artificial intelligence, we are summoning the demon" — referencing cautionary folklore to convey his concerns about uncontrolled AI development — a rhetorical device, not a disclosure of occult intent. Treating metaphor as confession is not evidence-based reasoning. Second, the theory requires a conspiracy of implausible scale: the claim that every major AI company, multiple government regulatory bodies, intelligence agencies, and executive-branch actors are all secretly coordinating alien contact protocols while publishing millions of pages of technical documentation, submitting to congressional testimony, and releasing auditable commercial products demands a level of coordinated silence that has no historical precedent and no supporting mechanism. Third, the claim is structurally unfalsifiable: any countervailing evidence — OpenAI's published charter, its research papers, its product releases, regulatory filings — is preemptively dismissed as part of the cover story, meaning the theory cannot in principle be disconfirmed by any observable fact. That is not a property of true claims; it is the defining property of unfalsifiable ones.

The theory does draw on a real and legitimate source of public concern: there is genuine unease about the opacity of large AI companies, the pace of development relative to oversight, and the appropriateness of government-industry relationships in the AI sector. Conversations around AI safety, oversight, and long-term impact are becoming more prominent, and some current and former insiders have publicly expressed caution about how quickly systems are evolving relative to governance structures. Those concerns deserve serious attention. But the portal theory exploits this legitimate anxiety by replacing institutional accountability — which requires engagement with verifiable facts, regulatory mechanisms, and documented behavior — with an unfalsifiable supernatural framework that forecloses rather than enables scrutiny. The effect is to redirect genuine concern about AI governance into a narrative that cannot be investigated, contested, or acted on, leaving believers disoriented and less equipped, not more, to engage meaningfully with the real issues at stake.

The direct harms of this specific theory remain at a low tier. There are no documented incidents of violence, harassment campaigns, or identifiable victims directly attributable to it. Its primary damage is epistemic: by misrepresenting a metaphorical quote as a literal admission and inventing a New Yorker source that does not exist, it models the core disinformation technique of fabricating journalistic authority to launder unverifiable claims. For journalists and content moderators, the evidentiary checklist is straightforward: the named source article does not exist, the named insider is unidentified, OpenAI's documented output is entirely inconsistent with the claimed purpose, and the quoted language is demonstrably figurative when examined in its original context.
