Voice of Reason

The claim circulating under the name "Aegon Chilakian" — a phonetic misspelling of A. Egon Cholakian — asserts that Earth's core is in imminent crisis and that humanity has only a few years to prevent planetary self-destruction. The claim calls on world leaders to act urgently and frames itself as suppressed scientific truth from a credentialed expert.

The first problem is with the messenger and his institutional context. A. Egon Cholakian describes himself as a retired civil law and particle physicist with a national security background, whose current professional activities center on U.S. and EU governmental and legislative affairs. Those credentials, while real in some measure, do not constitute expertise in geophysics, planetary science, or Earth's interior dynamics — the fields that would be necessary to substantiate any claim about the planet's core. More importantly, the context in which Cholakian has made the planetary-destruction claim is deeply compromised. Investigators at Babel.ua found that Cholakian spoke on the topic of the imminent end of the world at an online conference called "Global Crisis. There is a Way Out," conducted by an organization called Creative Society. Creative Society positions itself as an international volunteer movement, but it is in fact a rebranding of the AllatRa organization, which emerged in the early 2010s in Ukraine before opening offices in Russia, Kazakhstan, Moldova, the United States, and EU countries. Cholakian is a registered federal lobbyist, a registered foreign agent with the U.S. Department of Justice, and currently serves as the Capitol Hill representative of AllatRa. His apocalyptic warnings, in other words, are not independent scientific findings: they are advocacy materials produced on behalf of an organization that multiple governments and independent investigators have scrutinized as a pseudo-scientific cult. AllatRa's own president formally asked Cholakian to register as a foreign agent in the United States on the organization's behalf.

The specific scientific claim — that Earth is on the brink of self-destruction due to a failing core or a volcanic plume — lacks any corroboration from the planetary science community. In his address, Cholakian said that according to his observations, something bad is happening with Earth's core and there are only a few years before the planet self-destructs, and that efforts must be united among politicians and scientists to prevent this. No peer-reviewed study, no geological agency, and no credible scientific institution has corroborated any version of this timeline. The claim uses the format of scientific language — appeals to data, geophysics, and urgency — without the substance of peer review, reproducibility, or independent verification. This is a textbook feature of pseudoscience: it borrows scientific vocabulary while evading every mechanism that would allow the claim to be tested or refuted. Conspiracy theories of this type are usually resistant to falsification either by evidence against them or by a lack of evidence for them, and are reinforced by circular reasoning in which both evidence against the conspiracy and the absence of evidence for it are misinterpreted as evidence of its truth.

The rhetorical architecture of this claim also conforms perfectly to a centuries-long pattern of failed doomsday predictions. For centuries, doomsdayers have prophesied the apocalypse, but there is a catch: none of the end-of-world predictions have ever come true, and there have been hundreds through the ages. Every few years, or even every few months depending on which corners of the internet one frequents, a new story, speculation, or conspiracy will go viral claiming that the end of the world is near. The "few years left" framing is particularly durable as a manipulation tool precisely because it is close enough to feel urgent but far enough to be unfalsifiable in the short term. When the deadline passes without catastrophe, the prediction simply resets. "It's clear that these kinds of scenarios return over and over and over again," said John Hoopes, an archaeologist at the University of Kansas who has studied doomsday predictions. The Cholakian/AllatRa version adds a distinctive operational layer: dozens of individuals sent coordinated messages to news outlets demanding publication of Cholakian's "sensational address" to Biden, Xi Jinping, and Vladimir Putin. This coordinated pressure campaign — pushing the apocalyptic claim through mainstream media channels — is not how genuine scientific findings are disseminated; it is how influence operations work.

There is a kernel of genuine concern embedded in this narrative, and that is worth acknowledging honestly. Real risks to humanity from climate change, geopolitical conflict, and natural disasters are documented and serious. A United Nations report has warned that humanity's faulty perception of risk has set us on a "spiral of self-destruction," and that we face an onslaught of daily disasters by 2030 if risk management does not improve. Legitimate scientists do study long-term existential threats. But the AllatRa network exploits this legitimate anxiety by substituting a falsifiable, evidence-based risk conversation with an unfalsifiable, authority-leveraging panic narrative that serves the organization's recruitment and influence goals. When authorities in Russia and Ukraine began proceedings against AllatRa, Cholakian labeled those actions as "conspiratorial practices" and lobbied to establish the movement's international presence, accusing Russian security services and other bodies of conspiracy. Every critical voice is preemptively absorbed into the theory as evidence of suppression — the classic self-sealing move that makes the claim immune to correction. Journalists and content moderators encountering this narrative should treat the coordinated amplification campaign, the lobbying role of the named "scientist," and the unfalsifiable urgency framing as the primary red flags, irrespective of whatever real-world anxieties about planetary health the messaging is designed to exploit.
