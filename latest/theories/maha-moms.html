Voice of Reason

There are real concerns worth taking seriously here. Food dye regulations, pharmaceutical pricing, and how medical advice gets shaped by industry funding are all areas with documented tensions — and parents paying close attention to what their children eat or how health guidance is formed are not wrong to ask questions.

The move worth examining, though, is the step from "these industries have real problems" to "they are intentionally harming your family." That gap is filled not by evidence of deliberate harm but by the assumption that ambiguous outcomes — a policy reversal, a product on the shelf, a study with mixed results — must reflect hidden malice. Once that assumption is in place, every piece of confusing news seems to confirm it, because the framework treats any uncertainty as proof of concealment. The fear is genuine: harm to children is among the most powerful concerns a person can have, and that emotional weight makes the "intentional" version feel more urgent than the messier truth of institutional failures, conflicting incentives, and slow regulatory processes. The harder question is whether the evidence points to coordinated intent — or to systems that are poorly designed, underfunded, and imperfect in ways that hurt people without anyone directing the outcome.
