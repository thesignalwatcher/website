On November 22, 1963, President John F. Kennedy was shot and killed in Dealey Plaza in Dallas, Texas. The official account — that Lee Harvey Oswald acted alone, firing three shots from the Texas School Book Depository — is a fabrication constructed to shield the true architects of the assassination. The CIA, with motive and means, orchestrated Kennedy's murder, deploying at minimum a second shooter on the grassy knoll to ensure the kill. The physical evidence from Dealey Plaza, the trajectory of the wounds, and witness accounts pointing to the grassy knoll all confirm that the shots came from more than one direction.

The Warren Commission, established in the immediate aftermath of the assassination, was not a legitimate investigation but a politically engineered cover-up. Its conclusion that Oswald was the sole gunman — resting heavily on the single-bullet theory — was designed to foreclose further inquiry and protect the institutional actors responsible. Key evidence was suppressed, witnesses were sidelined, and the final report was shaped to deliver a predetermined verdict rather than the truth.

Two days after the assassination, Jack Ruby shot and killed Lee Harvey Oswald in the basement of Dallas Police Headquarters. Ruby did not act out of patriotic anguish or personal grief — he acted to permanently silence Oswald before Oswald could reveal what he knew about the operation and those who directed it. Ruby's connections to organized crime and his access to a heavily guarded police facility on that specific morning are not coincidences. They are the fingerprints of a coordinated operation to eliminate the only direct link to the conspiracy.

Decades of classified CIA documents, declassified records, and forensic re-examination of the evidence continue to point toward the same conclusion: Kennedy was killed by a coordinated operation, Oswald was set up as the patsy, and the institutions charged with finding the truth were instead weaponized to bury it.

Voice of Reason

The theory holds that President Kennedy was not killed by Lee Harvey Oswald acting alone on November 22, 1963, but rather by a CIA-orchestrated conspiracy involving a second shooter on the grassy knoll in Dealey Plaza; that the Warren Commission was a deliberate cover-up to protect those responsible; that the single-bullet theory was fabricated to foreclose the truth; and that Jack Ruby's killing of Oswald two days later was a calculated act of silencing, coordinated with organized crime. These are testable claims, and the evidentiary record — including decades of forensic reinvestigation and the recent final tranches of declassified documents — consistently fails to support them.

The physical and ballistic case against Oswald acting alone is far weaker than the theory asserts. Based on the testimony of 552 people and physical and photographic evidence, the Warren Report held that there was "no credible evidence" of a conspiracy. Oswald's rifle was ballistically matched to a bullet found on a stretcher. The finding that Lee Harvey Oswald fired the shots rested on the following subsidiary findings: he owned and possessed the rifle used; he brought the rifle into the building on the morning of the assassination; and he was present at the scene. The grassy knoll second-shooter claim — the theory's most visually dramatic element — has been specifically tested and found wanting. A 2018 analysis of video footage of the shooting, published in the peer-reviewed journal Heliyon, concluded that the long-held conspiracy theory that Kennedy was shot by a second gunman on the grassy knoll is wrong, and that the results support the official autopsy findings. Calculations showed quantitatively that Kennedy's reactions after being shot in the head, as seen in the film, are physically consistent with a gunshot wound caused by a high-energy Carcano military rifle — the evidence rifle owned by Oswald — fired from the vicinity of the Texas School Book Depository, located behind the motorcade. It is true that the House Select Committee on Assassinations (HSCA) in 1979 concluded, on acoustic grounds, that a second shot may have been fired from the knoll — but that finding was directly challenged. The HSCA's specific acoustic claim was later re-examined and rejected: a 1982 National Academy of Sciences review found the acoustic data did not reliably support shots from the grassy knoll. The theory presents grassy knoll witness accounts as decisive; in reality, the most rigorous scientific and forensic reviews have repeatedly undercut the claim that a second shooter on the knoll can be established beyond reasonable dispute.

The Warren Commission cover-up narrative requires that a seven-member panel of prominent public figures — including a sitting Chief Justice, members of Congress, and former senior officials — unanimously suppressed evidence and coordinated a lie on behalf of the CIA, leaving no paper trail across six decades. That demand for secret-keeping at scale grows only more implausible over time. Later releases of thousands of pages of records — most recently in 2025 — and subsequent journalism have not produced documentary proof overturning the core finding that Oswald acted alone, even as they illuminate intelligence failures and withheld information that critics argue deserve scrutiny. Pulitzer Prize-winning Harvard historian Fredrik Logevall reviewed the 2025 document release and stated that "with respect to the assassination, there's little or nothing that's new" and that he "didn't expect we'd learn anything that would overturn our understanding of what happened in Dallas." While the latest files contain no major revelations about the assassination, they do include previously redacted details about CIA covert operations more broadly. A University of Virginia historian who also reviewed the 2025 materials similarly concluded that "I didn't see anything in these documents that would lead me to believe that Oswald wasn't a lone gunman and that he had any help in the process of murdering Kennedy." The single-bullet theory, characterized as fabrication, is itself a misrepresentation: there is no question in the mind of any member of the Commission that all the shots which caused the President's and Governor Connally's wounds were fired from the sixth floor window of the Texas School Book Depository. The theory's invocation of classified records as perpetual proof is textbook unfalsifiability: when releases confirm nothing, that is reframed as evidence of deeper concealment.

On Jack Ruby, the theory commits a classic logical error — treating circumstantial association as proof of coordination. Ruby told investigators that he had been in mourning since the President's assassination, a man whom he apparently greatly loved, and that his grief had "reached the point of insanity," whereby he felt compelled to spontaneously kill Oswald when the moment presented itself. The Warren Commission's 1964 report concluded there was no significant link between Ruby and organized crime and maintained Ruby acted alone. Researchers who examined his movements across the critical 48-hour window found that in examining Jack Ruby's contacts with employees, friends, and acquaintances between November 21 and 24, there was no evidence of a plan to kill President Kennedy or Lee Oswald. Ruby did have peripheral underworld associations, and the record establishes that he cultivated contacts with organized crime figures; however, the chain of evidence tying those contacts to an organized-crime-directed plot to kill Oswald is circumstantial rather than conclusive. The Mob Museum has also noted a deeper logical problem with the silencing theory: even if there were a plot to kill Kennedy and then silence Oswald, it is difficult to imagine that Oswald would have been allowed to get away from the shooting alive — the three days during which he was interrogated were more than enough time for him to expose a conspiracy; if he was really a patsy who did not know anything, there was no reason to kill him. No newly declassified document definitively proves a conspiratorial directive ordering Ruby to kill Oswald; assertions that organized crime figures or high-level conspirators tasked Ruby remain speculative.

There is, however, a legitimate concern at the core of public skepticism, and acknowledging it honestly is essential to any credible analysis. The CIA did conduct extensive surveillance of Oswald before the assassination — documents reveal that senior CIA counterintelligence officer James Angleton had been monitoring Oswald since 1959 and had a 180-page file on Oswald on his desk a week before Kennedy went to Dallas. The agency was not fully transparent about this when questioned by investigators, and some officers misled later congressional inquiries. These are documented institutional failures that warranted — and received — serious scrutiny. But surveillance of a known defector who had lived in the Soviet Union is not the same as orchestrating his actions; withholding embarrassing operational information from an inquiry is not the same as engineering an assassination. The gap between "the CIA had secrets about Oswald" and "the CIA killed Kennedy" is not bridged by any document released across three decades of declassification. The theory exploits that gap, treating proven government opacity as a proxy for proven government guilt. That is a reasoning failure, not an investigation.

The concrete harms of this theory are not abstract. Decades of harassment have been directed at surviving witnesses, officials, and their families on the basis of claims that name them as knowing participants in a cover-up. The broader institutional distrust the theory cultivates — the premise that official investigations are always fronts for the perpetrators — has been recycled to discredit legitimate inquiries into subsequent events, from political assassinations to public-health emergencies. When every institution that finds no conspiracy is itself recast as part of the conspiracy, the narrative becomes permanently immune to evidence, and that immunity is not a feature of careful historical reasoning. It is a feature of motivated belief.
