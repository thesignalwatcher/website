Voice of Reason

This conspiracy theory clusters two distinct narratives: first, that Iran's strikes on U.S. military installations in Arab countries destroyed radar systems secretly used to suppress rainfall across the Middle East, and that the subsequent appearance of rainfall proved deliberate American weather engineering; second, that Jewish interests burned Patagonian forests in Argentina in a coordinated land-grab enabled by President Javier Milei's deregulation of foreign land ownership. Both claims are false, and the factual record dismantles each of them thoroughly.

On the conflict itself: the real armed confrontation was not a "40-day war" between Iran and a coalition of the United States, Israel, and six Arab countries. What Al Jazeera reported was a two-week ceasefire that halted 40 days of U.S.-Israeli attacks on Iran — meaning the "40 days" refers to the duration of strikes against Iran, not a war initiated by Iran against a coalition. The 2026 Iran war began on February 28, 2026, when the United States and Israel launched large-scale strikes on Iran, which included the assassination of Supreme Leader Khamenei. According to Iranian authorities, Iranian retaliatory attacks targeted American interests and military bases in the region in response to strikes by the United States and Israel that targeted Iran. The conspiracy theory inverts the basic timeline and belligerency: Iran was not the initiating aggressor against a coalition; it was the party receiving the initial major strike and retaliating. More critically, no credible source — military, journalistic, or governmental — has reported that any radar station destroyed in these exchanges served a weather-suppression function. Radar systems at military bases are used for air defense, missile tracking, and surveillance. The U.S. government's own National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration states directly: no technology exists that can create, destroy, modify, strengthen, or steer hurricanes in any way, and all such phenomena are natural. Technological limitations, inconsistent results, and a lack of evidence for large-scale manipulation underscore that cloud seeding influences only localized weather and does not enable broad control. The scientific consensus is that even the most optimistic applications of weather modification — cloud seeding using dispersed agents — can coax at best modest additional precipitation out of pre-existing clouds, and radar is a passive sensing tool, not an energy source capable of suppressing regional rainfall. No peer-reviewed physics, no government document, and no independent observer has ever described a mechanism by which a military radar installation could suppress precipitation across an entire region. The claim that rain returning to parts of the Middle East "proved" the radar was responsible is pure post hoc reasoning: weather is variable, the Middle East has experienced cyclical wet and dry years for millennia, and correlation between an unrelated military event and a weather observation requires an established mechanism — which is entirely absent here.

The Patagonian fire narrative is similarly built on real events twisted beyond recognition. On January 5, 2026, arsonists set fire to Patagonia's native forests, triggering massive blazes that scorched up to 52,000 acres in Argentina's Chubut province. Prosecutor Carlos Díaz Mayer confirmed that "the fire was started with an accelerant or petrol, which indicates that someone deliberately set the fire." So arson is confirmed for at least one fire — that part is accurate. But the theory's attribution of that arson to "Jewish interests" pursuing a land-acquisition scheme is unsupported fabrication. The chief prosecutor of Lago Puelo, Carlos Díaz Mayer, stated in a press conference that the main line of investigation is that the fire was deliberately started in the context of a land dispute between groups who live in the area — not foreign actors, not a coordinated ethnic conspiracy. As for the land-policy "trigger": Milei's Decree No. 70 included the repeal of the Rural Land Law and removal of restrictions on the acquisition of rural land by foreign persons as of December 29, 2023 — over two years before the fires, not days before them. Moreover, that repeal is frozen pending Supreme Court review, meaning the law remains enforced until a final ruling is issued. The December 9, 2025 date cited in the theory refers to a government press conference, not the original deregulation decree, which had been legally suspended in any case. The theory requires a criminal conspiracy that purchased or plotted to purchase burned land, was timed to a legal announcement, and left no financial, testimonial, or forensic evidence — and the actual prosecutor specifically contradicts the foreign-conspiracy framing.

Both halves of this composite narrative exploit genuine underlying anxieties. Concern about foreign land acquisition in sovereign states is a legitimate political debate in Argentina, where the Land Law, passed in 2011, limited the acquisition of rural land by foreigners with the aim of protecting national sovereignty, and its repeal by the Milei administration reignited a central debate about whether such restrictions defend national interests or hinder economic development. Similarly, questions about U.S. military presence in the Middle East and its regional consequences are real geopolitical concerns. But the theory hijacks both of these legitimate conversations and replaces evidence with a totalizing logic in which any weather event confirms secret engineering and any fire confirms a foreign ethnic plot. That unfalsifiability is the hallmark of conspiratorial reasoning: there is no piece of evidence — no dry month, no wet month, no fire with local suspects — that the theory cannot absorb as further confirmation. The concrete harm of this framing is real: several figures from Argentina's opposition shared claims circulating on social media accusing Israeli tourists of deliberately starting the fires, with one prominent figure accusing "a foreign state" of intentionally igniting them. Viral accounts by residents accused IDF soldiers allegedly visiting as tourists of starting the fires. Attributing a domestic land-dispute arson to an ethnic group on the basis of a fabricated timeline incites discrimination and deflects investigative resources away from the actual suspects. Readers encountering this theory should ask the single most effective diagnostic question: what evidence, if found, would convince the theory's proponents they were wrong? The answer, by design, is nothing — and that is the clearest signal that what is being offered is not analysis but propaganda.
