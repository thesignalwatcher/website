Voice of Reason

The theory under scrutiny holds that ancient beings of superhuman stature — identified with the biblical Nephilim — once walked the earth, that their skeletal remains have since been excavated across North America and elsewhere, and that the Smithsonian Institution, acting in concert with government agencies and mainstream media, has deliberately seized and destroyed this physical evidence to protect an official story of human origins.

Every factual pillar supporting this claim has been documented, traced to its origin, and found to be either a fabrication or a misrepresentation of genuinely complicated but well-understood history. The primary engine of the coverup story — the claim that the Smithsonian "admitted" to destroying thousands of giant skeletons — traces directly to a single December 2014 article on World News Daily Report. That article, titled "Smithsonian Admits to Destruction of Thousands of Giant Human Skeletons in Early 1900's," alleged that a Supreme Court ruling had forced the Smithsonian to release classified papers proving its involvement in a major historical coverup. Reuters determined that the origin of the story was a satirical website, and a spokesperson for the Smithsonian confirmed it was not true. No such Supreme Court decision exists, and if it did, it would have been a matter of public record widely reported in mainstream publications. The organization cited as the accuser — the "American Institution of Alternative Archeology" — is equally fictional: searches for it point back either to the article itself or to other pages referencing it, a strong indicator the organization does not exist. The circulating photographs that supposedly document giant skeletons are similarly fraudulent. The most viral image was created by a Canadian digital artist who entered it in a 2002 online photo-manipulation contest called "Archaeological Anomalies 2," using a real mastodon excavation photo as a base — and it has driven persistent internet rumors since 2004. Other widely shared images purporting to show giant skulls unearthed by paleontologists in Greece are altered versions of a 1993 University of Chicago photograph taken in Niger, Africa, during the excavation of a long-neck dinosaur. When conspiracy proponents cannot produce a single original, independently verified, peer-reviewed physical specimen — only photographs later proven to be digital contest entries and news articles from now-defunct satirical outlets — the evidentiary chain is not merely weak; it does not exist.

Beyond the fraudulent specific "documents," the theory fails at the level of basic physical science. As biologist J.B.S. Haldane demonstrated in his seminal 1928 essay, a humanoid figure sixty feet high would weigh roughly one thousand times as much as a normal person, meaning every square inch of bone would need to bear ten times the normal load — well past the fracture threshold of human bone with every step. An animal twice as tall experiences leg bones whose cross-sectional area is only four times as large while its weight becomes eight times as large, meaning muscles, bones, and organs could not scale concurrently to support the body. Galileo himself articulated this biological limit in 1638, noting that nature "cannot produce a giant ten times taller than an ordinary man unless by miracle," as the figure would "fall and be crushed under his own weight." The conspiracy requires believers to accept not only a coordinated institutional fraud spanning two centuries, but also the suspension of laws of physics that were established centuries before the Smithsonian was founded. The theory also cannot be falsified on its own terms: any absence of evidence is reinterpreted as evidence of a more thorough suppression, making the claim permanently immune to disproof — a defining mark of pseudoscience rather than inquiry.

There is, however, a real and legitimate institutional failure embedded in this history, and acknowledging it is essential to understanding why the conspiracy theory has proven durable. The Smithsonian's Bureau of Ethnology did actively encourage those excavating mounds to send Native American bones to its Mound Exploration Division, and prior to the passage of the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act in 1990, the Smithsonian collected over 18,000 such skeletons. Well into the 20th century, private collectors, museums, and federal agencies created vast collections of Native American human remains unearthed during expeditions on tribal homelands, which were scientifically examined and put on display — an act Native peoples experienced as a profound desecration of their ancestors. This is a documented, serious wrong. Precisely because the Smithsonian worked to debunk claims of an "ancient white race" as the Mound Builders, a conspiracy theory arose that Smithsonian archaeologists were destroying giants' bones to cover up evidence — inverting the institution's actual role as a debunker into the role of suppressor. The New Republic has further reported that in 2001, a writer produced an article titled "Holocaust of Giants: The Great Smithsonian Cover-Up," linking the giant claims explicitly to conservative evangelical Christianity, and that the overarching theme animating this alternative history movement was a longing for an imagined past when a rapidly diversifying United States was still mostly white and Christian. The grievance — that powerful institutions have treated indigenous remains without dignity and have not always been transparent — is real and was formally acknowledged by Congress. The solution to that grievance, enacted through NAGPRA and the National Museum of the American Indian Act, involves the Smithsonian having worked to return remains since 1984 and having offered the return of approximately 7,000 Native American individuals' remains since 1989, of which about 5,000 have been repatriated. The conspiracy theory hijacks that genuine history and redirects its energy not toward Indigenous rights but toward the fabricated claim of a lost race of biblical giants — a narrative that ultimately serves to erase, rather than honor, Native American history by imagining a pre-indigenous "white giant" civilization that supposedly preceded them.

The concrete harms flow directly from the theory's structure. Conspiracists continue to spread this lore across right-wing media platforms, and mainstream conservative politicians continue to escalate attacks on the Smithsonian — attacks that now threaten funding for genuine scientific and historical research. Believers who accept the theory's epistemology — that institutional silence means suppression and that the absence of evidence proves a more complete crime — are trained to distrust all expert consensus on the same reasoning, making them systematically more vulnerable to medical, scientific, and historical misinformation. And the theory's racially coded subtext, in which European settlers interpreted signs of cultural complexity in North American ruins as signs of whiteness in their creators, continues to deny Indigenous peoples authorship of their own archaeological heritage — a harm that is neither mythological nor ancient.
