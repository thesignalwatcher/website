The MMR vaccine triggers autism spectrum disorder in children, and this connection has been documented and then systematically buried. Pharmaceutical companies, facing enormous liability and profit consequences if the link became publicly accepted, have funded and shaped research to obscure the causal relationship. The result is a body of official literature that whitewashes what is actually happening to children after vaccination.

The CDC's role goes beyond passive complicity. The agency actively destroyed evidence of the vaccine-autism connection, ensuring that internal data linking the MMR vaccine to autism never reached the public or independent researchers. This destruction of evidence represents a deliberate institutional decision to protect the vaccine program at the expense of children's health and parental informed consent.

Vaccine injury is not the rare edge case that health authorities present. It is widespread, underreported, and deliberately minimized through reporting systems designed to make attribution difficult. Families who have watched their children regress after vaccination are told their observations are coincidental. The suppression operates across regulatory agencies, pharmaceutical manufacturers, and the medical establishment simultaneously, ensuring that no single point of accountability can be identified and pressured.

Voice of Reason

The claim under examination is that the MMR vaccine causes autism spectrum disorder, that this link has been deliberately buried by pharmaceutical companies and government regulators, and that the CDC actively destroyed internal evidence of the connection. Each of these assertions fails against the documented record.

The theory's evidentiary foundation is Andrew Wakefield's 1998 paper in The Lancet. Published on February 28, 1998, the paper — primarily authored by Wakefield and twelve co-authors — falsely claimed causative links between the MMR vaccine and colitis, and between colitis and autism. What the theory presents as a suppressed finding is in fact a documented fraud. Wakefield fabricated evidence to suggest a new "syndrome" he called "autistic enterocolitis," and at the time held a patent to a rival measles vaccine while being employed by a lawyer representing parents in lawsuits against vaccine producers, reportedly earning up to $43 million per year selling diagnostic kits for the syndrome he claimed to have discovered. Britain's General Medical Council found that Wakefield and two colleagues acted "dishonestly" and "irresponsibly," that the 12 children in the study had been specifically selected rather than consecutively referred as claimed, and that the study had not been approved by the appropriate ethics committee. The Lancet fully retracted the paper in February 2010, and Wakefield was censured and stripped of his British medical license. Ten of his twelve co-authors had already distanced themselves from the paper's interpretation years earlier. The original study was not suppressed evidence; it was exposed misconduct.

The scientific record that followed is not a whitewash — it is a coordinated research effort involving independent investigators across multiple countries, none of whom have financial stakes in the outcome aligning with each other. Following the publication of Wakefield's paper, numerous large-scale studies around the world involving hundreds of thousands of children have consistently found no evidence to support the claim that vaccines cause autism. A study published in The New England Journal of Medicine in 2002 examined data from 537,303 Danish children — 82% of whom had received the MMR vaccine — and found no evidence of increased autism risk among vaccinated children, even after controlling for age, sex, family income, birth weight, and gestational age; the timing of the vaccine made no difference either. A 2015 JAMA study analyzed data from over 95,000 children and found no increased risk of autism spectrum disorder associated with the MMR vaccine at any age, regardless of whether the child had an older sibling with autism. A landmark 2019 study conducted in Denmark followed over 650,000 children and found no increased risk of autism in those who received the MMR vaccine compared to those who did not. The thimerosal hypothesis — an alternative version of the claim, arguing that a mercury-based preservative in vaccines caused autism — has fared no better. After reviewing over 200 scientific studies examining thimerosal-containing vaccines and autism, the Institute of Medicine concluded that the studies "consistently provided evidence of no association between thimerosal-containing vaccines and autism." Critically, autism rates actually rose after thimerosal was removed from childhood vaccines — the opposite of what the hypothesis predicts, and a direct natural experiment disconfirming it.

The conspiracy claim rests primarily on two named pieces of alleged evidence, both of which collapse under scrutiny. The first is the CDC "whistleblower" case involving William Thompson, a CDC researcher who in 2014 alleged that data about African American boys were omitted from a 2004 CDC-published study. The theory inflates this into proof that the CDC destroyed evidence of a universal vaccine-autism link. What Thompson's own statement actually said was narrower: he acknowledged that he and his colleagues omitted data from a 2004 article in Pediatrics that "suggested that African American males who received the MMR vaccine before age 36 months were at increased risk for autism," and that he believed the final study protocol was not followed. Independent reviews of his claims have shown there is no evidence of an increase in autism arising from the full dataset when properly analyzed. The reanalysis published by Brian Hooker — the anti-vaccine advocate who recorded Thompson's phone calls — was itself retracted. Hooker's paper was taken down in August 2014 and formally retracted in October 2014. The alleged subgroup finding, even taken at face value, concerns a specific statistical subset in a single study — not a universal vaccine-autism mechanism — and has not been replicated across the vast independent literature. The second named piece of evidence is the case of Poul Thorsen, a Danish researcher who co-authored key vaccine-safety studies. He has been charged with stealing over $1 million in CDC grant money and faces 13 counts of wire fraud and 9 counts of money laundering. He was arrested in June 2025 during a vacation in Germany and extradited to the United States. This is genuine financial fraud, and the indictment is real — but the theory's inference that Thorsen's embezzlement taints the science is a logical non-sequitur. Stealing grant money does not falsify the data the grant produced; the CDC has never withdrawn the studies; and multiple independent replications have reached the same conclusion. The fraud case and the science case are separate. Thorsen was one of many authors on these papers and was not the lead author on any of them.

There is a legitimate grievance embedded in this theory's appeal. Vaccine adverse events are real, they are documented, and public health institutions have at times communicated risk poorly. The low doses of thimerosal in vaccines may be associated with minor side effects such as redness and swelling at the injection site, and rare allergic reactions. Regulatory agencies have faced genuine conflicts of interest and real failures of transparency in other domains. Parents who notice developmental changes in their children around the time of vaccination are observing something real — the timing of early childhood vaccination overlaps almost exactly with the developmental window in which autism signs typically become apparent, making temporal correlation nearly inevitable regardless of cause. These are legitimate concerns that deserve honest scientific engagement. What the theory does is exploit them to substitute a fabricated causal mechanism for a genuinely complex one, and to reframe every failed replication as further evidence of conspiracy. That move — in which refutation becomes confirmation — is the hallmark of an unfalsifiable belief system rather than a falsifiable scientific hypothesis.

Misinformation about vaccines has real-world consequences: decreased vaccination rates have led to outbreaks of preventable diseases, such as measles, which had been nearly eradicated in many countries, and when vaccine uptake declines, vulnerable populations — including children with neurodevelopmental conditions — face increased risks of severe illness. Wakefield's original assertion caused one of the biggest medical rows in a generation and led to a significant fall in the number of vaccinations, prompting a worrying rise in cases of measles. Children who contracted measles, suffered complications, or died in those outbreaks were the first victims of the original fraud. The theory does not protect children from a hidden danger; it exposes them to a documented one.
