Voice of Reason

The study most people point to here — Andrew Wakefield's 1998 Lancet paper — was fully retracted after an investigation found the data had been manipulated and that Wakefield held undisclosed financial conflicts of interest. The British Medical Journal published a detailed analysis concluding it was not merely flawed but fraudulent. That is the starting point, not a detail to set aside. Separately, thimerosal (a mercury-based preservative) was removed from childhood vaccines in the early 2000s as a precaution; autism diagnosis rates continued rising afterward, which is the opposite of what a causal link would predict.

The claim then asks you to read that ongoing rise as proof of a cover-up — treating the absence of a smoking gun as evidence the gun was hidden. But that move only holds if you first assume a coordinated deception across thousands of researchers, regulators, and clinicians worldwide, none of whom have broken ranks in decades. Once you accept that hidden machine, every ambiguous detail seems to click into place — that is the feeling of a pattern, not proof of one. Vaccines do carry real, documented risks; acknowledging that is not the same as accepting a causal link to autism that the evidence, repeatedly and independently tested, has not supported. Origins & History

The claim that the MMR vaccine causes autism traces to a specific, well-documented origin: a paper by Andrew Wakefield and twelve co-authors published in *The Lancet* on 28 February 1998, preceded by a press conference at the Royal Free Hospital, London, on 26 February. The paper, based on a case series of twelve children, proposed a link between MMR vaccination, a novel bowel disorder, and autism. Near-immediate tabloid amplification in the United Kingdom drove a measurable fall in MMR uptake during the late 1990s and early 2000s.

Investigative reporting by Brian Deer, published in *The Sunday Times* beginning in 2004, documented serious ethical and methodological problems with the study; *The Lancet* issued a partial retraction that year and a full retraction on 2 February 2010. The *BMJ* subsequently published Deer's findings characterizing the original data as fraudulent ([bmj.com/content/342/bmj.c5347](https://www.bmj.com/content/342/bmj.c5347); [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancet_MMR_autism_fraud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancet_MMR_autism_fraud)).

Despite retraction, the claim continued to circulate internationally, amplified from roughly 2007 onward by celebrity advocates in the United States and, later, by Wakefield's 2016 film *Vaxxed* and ongoing activism by figures including Robert F. Kennedy Jr. The claim also draws on older anti-vaccination traditions dating to nineteenth-century smallpox controversies and a parallel, partially overlapping scare concerning the preservative thimerosal. Search interest in the vaccine–autism connection has been documented to spike around disease outbreaks and public vaccine policy debates.
