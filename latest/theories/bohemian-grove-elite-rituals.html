Voice of the Believer

Deep in the heart of California, hidden away from the public eye, lies a mysterious, exclusive retreat where the world's most powerful figures gather — and what happens there is no accident, no harmless summer camp, no innocent fraternity prank.

Every summer, during the most charged weeks of the season, the most powerful global elites convene at Bohemian Grove for rituals that go far beyond anything you were told about. A notable feature of the Grove is a 40-foot concrete owl statue, which is central to the annual "Cremation of Care" ceremony performed by members — and that owl is not decoration. It is a symbol of who these men are and what they worship when the cameras are gone and the gates are locked. They've been doing it since the 1890s and nobody knew about it until the year 2000, and it's still not open to the public — which tells you everything you need to know about why they work so hard to keep it that way. This is where symbols like the owl and phrases like "Cremation of Care" carry deeper meanings related to power consolidation and control, coded in plain sight for those with eyes to see.

What gets decided inside those redwoods does not stay there. Bohemian Grove has been the birthplace of many different world-shaping events, everything from hand-selecting presidents and heads of state to possibly the creation of the first stealth bomber and the nuclear program that would lead to the end of WWII. These gatherings serve as a platform to reinforce elite influence and manipulate global events behind closed doors — this is not metaphor, this is mechanism. The Bohemian Club and everything that takes place within their personal Bohemian Grove is the operating layer beneath the governments and institutions you think are running the world.

None of this exists in isolation. The activities at Bohemian Grove are part of a broader conspiracy involving secret societies and the New World Order — the Grove is simply where the mask comes fully off, where the men who shape nations perform their secretive rituals and remind each other what they truly serve. We dive into the symbols of the club, the weird rituals they do, and the conspiracies that connect them outward to every power structure on earth. When you understand what the Cremation of Care ceremony actually signifies — the ritual burning away of conscience, of guilt, of any remaining bond to ordinary humanity — you understand why they need it. You cannot do what these men do to the world while your care is still intact.

Voice of Reason

The theory claims that a global elite gathers annually at California's Bohemian Grove to perform occult rituals — including sacrificial ceremonies before a giant owl idol — as part of a secret conspiracy to coordinate world domination outside of any democratic accountability.

The basic facts of Bohemian Grove are not disputed and are extensively documented. The Bohemian Club is an elite invitation-only social club founded in San Francisco in 1872 by male artists, writers, actors, lawyers, and journalists interested in arts and culture. The Bohemian Grove is a private campground covering 2,700 acres in Monte Rio, California, owned by the Club, which hosts an annual gathering lasting more than two weeks each mid-July, attended by invited members and guests from politics, business, and the arts. Notable members or guests have included Clint Eastwood, Henry Kissinger, Walter Cronkite, Richard Nixon, Ronald Reagan, and Charles Schwab. The centerpiece ceremony — the Cremation of Care — is equally real and equally well-documented. It is an annual ritual production written, produced, and performed by and for members of the Bohemian Club, staged at the Grove near Monte Rio in front of a 40-foot-tall image of an owl at a small artificial lake amid a private old-growth grove of redwood trees. What the ceremony actually consists of, however, is the decisive point: it is a theatrical, annual opening ritual originally created in the late 19th century to mark the start of the club's summer retreat and to symbolize setting aside worldly worries, involving a scripted pageant culminating in the burning of an effigy called "Dull Care." The ceremony was first conducted in 1881. The owl statue itself is not a deity or cult object — it is a 30-foot hollow concrete structure over steel supports designed by sculptor Haig Patigian, constructed in the late 1920s, and used as the backdrop for the ceremony since 1929. For many years, the recorded voice used during the ceremony as "The Owl" belonged to club guest Walter Cronkite. The most-cited "exposé" of the Grove — Alex Jones's July 2000 covert filming — actually confirms the theatrical interpretation rather than refuting it. Jones recorded the Cremation of Care, but his later claim that the ceremony involved "ritual sacrifice" is a characterization not supported by other reporting. Jon Ronson, the British journalist who accompanied Jones on the infiltration and watched the same footage, stated on PBS Frontline that Jones was "totally overselling the whole thing in a sort of irresponsible way," and that what he actually witnessed was a papier-mâché effigy — not a human being. Ronson's lasting impression was of "an all-pervading sense of immaturity: the Elvis impersonators, the pseudo-pagan spooky rituals, the heavy drinking," describing attendees as men who "emotionally seemed trapped in their college years."

The conspiracy version of these facts fails on every structural level. The "occult ritual" claim rests on aesthetics — robes, firelight, a large owl — rather than on any documented evidence of occult belief or practice among members. Connecting theatrical props to Canaanite or Luciferian religion, as Jones did, requires ignoring the ceremony's plainly documented 1881 origin as a whimsical Victorian theatrical tradition. At the time the script was developed, "care" (from Old English "cearu") was synonymous with anxiety or anguish — a more negative connotation than the word carries today — making "Cremation of Care" straightforwardly a ceremony about burning away worry. The "secret global coordination" claim faces an even more fundamental problem of mechanism: the Club's own motto, posted throughout the grounds, is "Weaving Spiders Come Not Here," expressing the expectation that outside concerns and business dealings be set aside, though discussions of business sometimes occur in private conversations. Even accepting that some informal policy discussions take place, that is categorically different from a coordinated, centralized shadow government. The membership itself — regular full members who must often wait 15 years for an opening, as the club limits itself to about 2,700 men — encompasses artists, musicians, academics, and military officers alongside politicians and executives, making any monolithic ideological coordination implausible. Finally, the claim is unfalsifiable by design: every piece of counter-evidence, from the mundane content of the ceremony to the lack of any corroborating testimony from the thousands of people who have attended over 145 years, gets absorbed into the theory as further proof of the conspiracy's success at concealment.

There is a legitimate concern at the theory's core. Club members have included U.S. presidents, many cabinet officials, and CEOs of large corporations including major financial institutions, with major military contractors, oil companies, banks, and national media having high-ranking officials as members or guests. The concentration of influential people in a private, press-excluded setting is a genuine transparency issue, and sociologists have studied the Grove as a mechanism of elite social cohesion. According to a memo from one of Ronald Reagan's assistants to Alan Greenspan, Reagan's 1983 cuts to Social Security were planned at a Bohemian Club meeting two years prior. These are substantive concerns about power, access, and the influence of exclusive networks — concerns that exist entirely independent of any occult interpretation and are far better addressed through journalism, lobbying reform, and transparency law than through rituals-and-owl mythology. The occult framing actively obstructs this legitimate scrutiny by making serious analysis of elite networking seem contaminated by association with extremist claims.

The harm produced by this theory is concrete and traceable. In January 2002, Richard McCaslin attempted an armed raid on the Bohemian Grove, later claiming he had been motivated by concern about ritualistic child sacrifice after watching Alex Jones's documentary, and further energized by 9/11 conspiracy theories. He entered the campground armed with a rifle-shotgun hybrid, a .45 caliber handgun, a crossbow, a two-foot-long sword, a knife, and a fireworks mortar tube. He was convicted on felony charges of arson, burglary, brandishing a weapon at a peace officer, and two counts for being armed while wearing a bulletproof vest. Upon entering, McCaslin found no evidence of the sacrificial rituals he believed he would interrupt. He died by suicide in 2018. Journalist Tea Krulos, who documented McCaslin's story in the 2020 book *American Madness*, described him as "Patient Zero of someone who listened to Jones and took his bloviating seriously." McCaslin's trajectory — from viewer of a sensationalized documentary to convicted armed felon to suicide — is a documented, real-world demonstration of what happens when theatrical pageantry is repackaged as evidence of child sacrifice, and when the gap between legitimate concern about elite power and delusional certainty about occult conspiracy is deliberately erased.
