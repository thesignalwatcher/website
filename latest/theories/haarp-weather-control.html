Voice of Reason

The HAARP weather-control theory holds that a U.S. government facility in Alaska is not a research program but a covert military weapons platform capable of engineering hurricanes, floods, and earthquakes on demand, deploying these engineered disasters as geopolitical weapons against rival nations or targeted populations.

Every factual premise that theory rests on is either false, radically misstated, or physically incoherent. HAARP — the High-frequency Active Auroral Research Program — is a scientific endeavor aimed at studying the properties and behavior of the ionosphere; its operation was transferred from the United States Air Force to the University of Alaska Fairbanks in August 2015. It is the world's most capable high-power, high-frequency transmitter for study of the ionosphere, with a principal instrument — the Ionospheric Research Instrument — consisting of a phased array of 180 crossed-dipole antennas spread across 33 acres and capable of radiating 3.6 megawatts into the upper atmosphere. Three-point-six megawatts sounds imposing until context is applied. When HAARP began operating, its 960-kilowatt output was approximately the same as that generated by ten AM radio stations. Even at its current maximum, the facility's practical effect on the ionosphere is modest and precisely characterized: transmissions from HAARP cause only small effects in the ionosphere that last a few seconds, and the facility operates for only a few hours each year; the amount of high-frequency energy coming from amateur radio operators around the world almost certainly exceeds transmissions from HAARP. The physical barrier between that modest ionospheric heating and surface weather is categorical, not a matter of degree. Former HAARP chief scientist Chris Fallen stated explicitly that HAARP cannot control the weather — it has too little power and affects a different part of the atmosphere. NOAA, the national authority on atmospheric science, is unambiguous on the same point: HAARP is a small National Science Foundation-funded facility that conducts research on the ionosphere 30 to 600 miles above Earth's surface and is not capable of influencing local weather at Earth's surface, let alone tropical cyclones thousands of miles away; the HAARP system is basically a large radio transmitter. The seismic claim is even less defensible. Earthquakes stem from crustal stress releases far below HAARP's reach; its radio waves do not penetrate rock, and the power involved is trivial compared to seismic energies. The HAARP program manager, Jessica Matthews, stated directly after the February 2023 Turkey earthquake — which conspiracy content blamed on the facility — that "the research equipment at the HAARP site cannot create or amplify natural disasters."

The theory's primary intellectual source is Nick Begich, whose 1995 book "Angels Don't Play This HAARP" argued that the HAARP facility features technology that could be used to control the weather, control minds, and change brain chemistry. Begich is not a physicist or atmospheric scientist, and the arguments in his books have been examined and found wanting: the arguments presented in the books of Nick Begich are found to contain multiple fallacies and lack relevant evidence, with the claims about HAARP's capabilities deemed unfounded and not convincing, as they are based on speculation rather than evidence. It is also worth noting that Begich acknowledged making over a million dollars from HAARP conspiracy claims over a twenty-year period — a commercial incentive that independent researchers should weigh. The HAARP conspiracy theory has spread with particular virulence in Central and South America, where the facility is regularly blamed for a variety of weather and seismic events; in 2010, Venezuelan President Hugo Chávez blamed a magnitude 5.6 earthquake on an American "earthquake weapon," with Venezuelan media suggesting he was referring to HAARP. The theory's invulnerability to any disconfirming evidence is itself the most important analytical flag: every major disaster anywhere on Earth can be retrofitted into the narrative, and the claim that engineered events are indistinguishable from natural ones by design is precisely the rhetorical device that places the theory permanently beyond the reach of evidence. No pattern of HAARP operation logs, no atmospheric physics, no seismological record, and no contrary event can falsify it — which is a description of magical thinking, not a description of a testable claim about the physical world.

The theory does tap into a legitimate concern: the original HAARP program was indeed funded by the Air Force and the Navy and did pursue research into ionospheric effects with potential military communications applications — including, as documented, research into using ionospheric heating to communicate with submarines. Since it opened, HAARP hosted scientists doing applied research for the military, including work using the antenna array to heat a part of the ionosphere to send an ocean-penetrating signal to a submarine; the military experiments and the complex nature of studying a region people cannot see or easily understand have fed a controversy about HAARP. Skepticism about dual-use military research programs is reasonable civic behavior. What the theory does with that kernel, however, is expand a verifiable and publicly documented research mission into an unfalsifiable global weapons system for which no independent physical evidence, no intercepted orders, no dissident whistleblower, and no anomalous atmospheric signature has ever been produced. The ionosphere sits 50 to 400 miles above the surface. The jet stream, where weather forms, lies between 6 and 9 miles up. The mechanism by which HAARP's brief, localized ionospheric heating could cascade down through more than 40 miles of atmosphere to steer a hurricane is never specified, because no such mechanism exists in physics.

The concrete harms from this theory are well-documented and ongoing. Viral social media posts blamed HAARP for Hurricane Helene in 2024, contributing to violent threats against first responders and meteorologists. Meteorologist James Spann reported an influx of threatening messages including messages like "stop lying about the government controlling the weather — or else." The spread of weather-related conspiracy theories takes a toll on the mental health of meteorologists and other professionals in the field, with a social scientist at NOAA describing them as "all traumatized." At a systemic level, officials have repeatedly warned that misinformation around storms imperils lives and hampers response efforts. When people are told that a hurricane is a government weapon, some refuse evacuation orders, others direct rage at emergency managers rather than seeking their help, and the deliberate misattribution of climate-intensified disasters to secret state actors displaces the public understanding needed to address the actual drivers of increasingly severe weather. The theory does not merely describe a world; it actively degrades the capacity of people to survive it.
