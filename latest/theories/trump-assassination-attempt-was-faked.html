Voice of Reason

The theory under examination holds that the July 13, 2024, Butler, Pennsylvania shooting was an orchestrated political spectacle, not a genuine assassination attempt, and that the FBI, consistent with its supposed role in manufacturing other crises, engineered or covered up the event to benefit Donald Trump electorally.

Every primary fact on record refutes this theory at its foundation. The shooting claimed the life of Buffalo Township resident and former fire chief Corey Comperatore, left campaign rally spectators James Copenaver and David Dutch critically injured, and sent Trump, who suffered a wound to his ear, for treatment at Butler Memorial Hospital. Thousands of people witnessed the event, which showed Trump visibly injured and resulted in an attendee being killed. Associated Press photojournalist Evan Vucci captured photographs of Trump with blood on his face and ear, pumping his fist in the air — images that were unrehearsed products of a chaotic scene, not a staged tableau. Fifteen recorded 911 calls were released by Butler County, with the first call coming in at 6:12 p.m., about a minute after shots rang out. Thomas Matthew Crooks, the 20-year-old who carried out the attack, acted entirely alone, according to findings that followed one of the most extensive investigations ever conducted into an attack on a U.S. political figure. The FBI's own forensic record shows that Crooks searched for ammonium nitrate and other explosive precursors, researched campaign events for both Trump and Biden in Western Pennsylvania, specifically searched for details of Trump's Butler event on July 4, 2024, and registered to attend the rally on July 6. He left no manifesto, no explanation for why he tried to kill the former and future president, and in the year since the shooting investigators have been piecing together what led him to climb that roof, with frustratingly few answers. A real conspiracy requires conspirators who can be found; here there are none.

The theory's portrait of the FBI as orchestrator is constructed almost entirely from innuendo and category error. The claim that the Bureau "lied about Crooks's online footprint" is not supported by any verified evidence; what exists is an unresolved debate over completeness of a toxicology report that does not approach evidence of staging. The broader assertion — that because the FBI used informants aggressively in the Whitmer kidnapping case and had informants present in crowds on January 6, it therefore staged a real shooting — is a logical non sequitur. The Whitmer case, built over nearly a year using as many as a dozen informants and two undercover agents across four states, has been used as fuel for conspiracy theories that the January 6 Capitol riot was engineered by the FBI, despite a decades-long public record proving the opposite. The Whitmer prosecution is a contested and genuinely complex case involving documented informant conduct — but even its fiercest critics have not established that the FBI stages mass-casualty events against the politicians it is protecting. The leap from "FBI has used informants in sting operations" to "FBI orchestrated a live shooting at a presidential campaign rally" is not an inference; it is an unfalsifiable assertion that requires ignoring the complete absence of any mechanism, document, witness, or physical evidence connecting the Bureau to Crooks's rifle.

The theory also misrepresents the institutional accountability process as evidence of cover-up. During her congressional testimony, Director Cheatle called the assassination attempt "the most significant operational failure at the Secret Service in decades," and stated before the House committee: "On July 13, we failed. As the director of the United States Secret Service, I take full responsibility for any security lapse." After failing to answer the most basic questions from lawmakers — including how the shooter got access to the roof and why the building wasn't part of the security perimeter — bipartisan calls for her resignation intensified. Following her testimony, House Oversight Committee Chairman James Comer and Ranking Member Jamie Raskin — a Republican and a Democrat — issued a joint statement calling on Cheatle to resign. This is what genuine bipartisan institutional pressure looks like, and it is the opposite of theater designed to foreclose inquiry. The resulting bipartisan congressional task force concluded that "the tragic events of July 13 were preventable," with preliminary findings based on 23 transcribed interviews, thousands of pages of documents, and a public hearing. Investigations that reach documented conclusions about preventable security failures are not shut down before completion; this one produced an interim and a final report, both publicly released.

There is a kernel of legitimate concern underneath the theory that this narrative absorbs and then distorts. The Secret Service did fail catastrophically on July 13. Multiple reports of a threat were made prior to Trump's appearance, and the shooter had been identified as a person of interest more than an hour before shots were fired. The FBI's use of informants in sting operations is a documented practice with documented abuses that warrants ongoing democratic scrutiny. These are real grievances about institutional competence and accountability. But the conspiracy theory converts them into an unfalsifiable closed loop: every piece of counter-evidence — the forensic record, the 911 calls, the dead attendee, the congressional findings, the director's resignation — is reinterpreted as further proof of the cover-up's sophistication. That structure, where no possible evidence could falsify the claim, is the hallmark of conspiratorial reasoning rather than empirical argument.

The documented harm is concrete and measurable. No evidence has surfaced to support the conspiracy theories claiming that the shooting was staged. Nevertheless, a poll conducted by NewsGuard/YouGov among approximately 1,000 Americans found that many respondents believe the assassination attempts against Trump were fake. Notably, the poll question bundled "staged" and "not sure" together in ways that inflated apparent belief in staging, and the partisan breakdown is striking: 24 percent of all respondents said they believed the Butler attempt was staged, including 42 percent of Democratic respondents compared with 7 percent of Republicans. The conspiracy theory has been persistent since minutes after Secret Service escorted a bleeding Trump off the stage, when social media lit up with claims the shooting was staged — arguing, for example, that Trump faked it with a "blood pill" and that photojournalists had been tipped off. The practical consequence of this misinformation is corrosive to political trust across the spectrum and deprives the public of the accurate understanding needed to demand genuine accountability for the real security failures that actually did occur and that a real investigation has already documented.
