Voice of Reason

When a president delegates day-to-day Situation Room access to senior staff and National Security Council advisers, that is standard practice, not evidence of exclusion. Every modern administration has career officials and appointed aides conducting preparatory briefings, drafting options, and managing communications before decisions reach the Oval Office. The question is whether presidential direction is present — not whether the president is physically in every room.

The leap here is reading normal institutional process as proof of hidden control. A deputy press secretary saying "all options are on the table" is a routine diplomatic formulation; treating it as unauthorized freelancing requires assuming bad intent first. Once that assumption is in place, any delegation looks like a takeover, any briefing looks like a bypass, and any absent president looks like a hostage to his own staff. That feeling of pieces clicking together is real — but it comes from the assumption, not from the evidence. The claim also requires a machine large enough that no missing proof can count against it: if no sources go on record, that becomes proof of suppression. A simpler read is that normal national security processes look opaque from the outside, and opacity is being mistaken for conspiracy.
