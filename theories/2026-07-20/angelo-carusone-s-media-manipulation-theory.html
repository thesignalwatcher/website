The lines are gone — the wall between right-wing media and the levers of government power has collapsed, and what we are watching now is a coordinated machine running misinformation straight from the White House into American living rooms.

Media Matters President Angelo Carusone has made this case with receipts. When Chris Hayes talks to Media Matters President Angelo Carusone about the explosive new audio his organization uncovered, what you are hearing is not punditry — it is documentation. The Trump administration has merged with the right-wing media to spread misinformation, and Fox News is the primary transmission line. Tucker Carlson, the anchors, the whole apparatus — they are not commentators on power, they are instruments of it. The lines between Fox News and the White House have been blurred, allowing for direct implementation of election lies into the broader media bloodstream.

And when the pressure comes, Fox does not correct course — it retreats into silence. Fox News is doing damage control by ignoring Trump's election lies, which is itself a choice, a deliberate editorial posture that protects the lie by refusing to name it. That silence is active. Carusone's organization exists precisely to expose this mechanism: to show how conspiracy theories move from fringe spaces onto mainstream platforms, laundered through credible-seeming anchors and prime-time slots until they become ambient, unquestioned, treated as common knowledge. The merger is the message.

Voice of Reason

The theory under examination holds that Angelo Carusone — president of the nonprofit media watchdog Media Matters for America — has himself been advancing a conspiratorial narrative: that the Trump administration secretly "colluded" with Fox News to spread election disinformation as part of a hidden, coordinated plot to undermine public trust in elections. Treating Carusone as a conspiracy theorist is the central inversion this entry addresses.

The first factual problem is that this framing misrepresents what Carusone actually said and who he is. Carusone is an American nonprofit executive who serves as president of Media Matters for America, a nonprofit media watchdog. He was named president and CEO in December 2016. His public statements about the Trump administration and right-wing media are not conspiracy theories — they are analytical commentary made on mainstream television, grounded in documented, verifiable facts about personnel and institutional behavior. The specific remark at issue, "the lines are gone," appeared during a television segment on MSNBC and referred explicitly to a concrete, named individual: Carusone was explaining, in the context of discussing Fox News and election-related claims, that the old feedback loop between media and government had changed. He pointed to John Solomon — identified as running a White House task force — as an example: someone who could appear on Steve Bannon's podcast in the morning and then walk into the White House to implement policy. "The lines are gone," he said, meaning the institutional separation had collapsed into personnel overlap, not that there was a hidden cabal directing talking points from a secret room. That is an empirical observation about staffing, not a secret coordination theory.

The empirical record underlying that observation is, in key respects, independently established. Among the highest-profile examples, Pete Hegseth, one of the hosts of Fox & Friends Weekend, became secretary of defense, and Dan Bongino, who had left Fox in 2023, became deputy director of the FBI. Sean Duffy, who had co-hosted a Fox Business program, became secretary of transportation, and his wife, Rachel Campos-Duffy, continued to host Fox & Friends Weekend. Trump also tapped Fox News host Jeanine Pirro to serve as interim U.S. attorney in Washington, D.C., and what Media Matters has called the "revolving door" between the network and the Trump White House also runs in the other direction, with family members and former White House staffers obtaining Fox roles. This is verifiable personnel data, not inference. More critically, the Dominion Voting Systems lawsuit, settled in 2023, produced a documentary record that directly dismantles the claim that Fox was coordinating with the White House to push election lies it believed in. The evidence showed precisely the opposite dynamic. Records released as part of the lawsuit showed how Fox hosts and executives did not believe the claims by Trump's allies but aired them anyway, in part to win back viewers who were fleeing the network after it correctly called Arizona for Democrat Joe Biden on election night. Sean Hannity said in deposition that he "did not believe it for one second" regarding the fraud claims; a February 2023 court filing showed primetime hosts messaging each other to insult and mock Trump advisers; and Rupert Murdoch himself messaged that Trump's voter fraud claims were "really crazy stuff," telling the Fox CEO it was "terrible stuff damaging everybody." Fox News agreed to pay Dominion $787.5 million — the largest known media defamation settlement in U.S. history — and acknowledged the court's earlier ruling that Fox had broadcast false statements about Dominion. This is not the profile of a network faithfully implementing White House messaging as a matter of ideology; it is the profile of a commercial institution that amplified lies for audience retention, privately contemptuous of the very claims it was broadcasting.

There is a legitimate concern at the core of what Carusone — and independent journalists including Jane Mayer of The New Yorker — have been documenting for years: the historically unusual porousness between a media organization and a presidential administration, the revolving-door personnel transfers, and the way ideologically symbiotic media ecosystems can amplify misinformation without requiring formal coordination. Jane Mayer reported that past administrations had favored press members, but there was "nothing where someone is so close in that they are coordinating on a daily basis with the president," a situation she had never encountered in any previous White House she had studied. That concern is legitimate, documented, and important. What transforms it into a distortion is the leap from documented institutional closeness and commercial misinformation to the claim that Carusone is advancing a secret-collusion narrative. He is not. He is describing observable structural facts and doing so as an identified media critic on attribution. The conspiracy theory reversal — casting the analyst as the conspiracist — is itself a recognizable disinformation tactic: targeting the messenger, disputing the framing of an observation to avoid engaging the substance of the evidence it rests on. Treating a documented revolving door as unfalsifiable speculation, and treating the person pointing to the door as the problem, is precisely the kind of reasoning failure that counter-disinformation research exists to name and correct.
