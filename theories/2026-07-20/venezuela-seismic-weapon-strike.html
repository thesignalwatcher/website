Every factual premise of this theory dissolves on contact with the actual record.

Start with the earthquake itself. The two quakes — a 7.2 magnitude one followed just seconds later by a 7.5 — struck the coast of Venezuela on Wednesday evening, knocking down buildings in Caracas. The geological explanation is straightforward and pre-existing. Northern Venezuela lies within a broad, transpressional boundary zone between the Caribbean Plate and the South American Plate. Its principal active structure is the Boconó–San Sebastián–El Pilar fault system, which extends for more than 1,300 km from the Venezuelan Andes to Trinidad and accommodates much of the dextral motion between the plates at about 10 mm per year. The USGS described the mainshock as shallow strike-slip faulting near the complex plate boundary along the San Sebastián fault system, consistent with the regional tectonic setting of east–west-trending right-lateral faults in northern Venezuela. This is not a novel attribution invented after the fact. A geophysicist at Florida International University noted the area was "overdue" for an earthquake, and he and his colleagues had calculated — and presented at a European Space Agency conference just the week before — that accumulated strain on the San Sebastián fault was capable of producing a 7.1 magnitude rupture. The fault, in other words, was independently flagged by researchers before the event, using standard strain-accumulation models. The same plate-boundary system has been associated with several large historical earthquakes in northern Venezuela, including events in 1641, 1766, 1812, 1900, and 1967. A weapon is not required to explain what a centuries-long seismic record already predicts.

Now for Trump's "secret weapon" statements, which form the theory's most visible factual hook. The actual record of what Trump said is specific and public. President Donald Trump said the U.S. used a secret weapon he called "The Discombobulator" to disable Venezuelan equipment when the U.S. captured Nicolás Maduro. Trump was commenting on reports that the U.S. had a pulsed energy weapon and said, "The Discombobulator. I'm not allowed to talk about it." He said the weapon made Venezuelan equipment "not work." Trump discussed the weapon's effectiveness during the operation on January 3, when Maduro and his wife were arrested at a compound in Caracas by U.S. personnel. The context is unambiguous: Trump was describing an electronic warfare or pulsed-energy system used to disable missile and weapons systems during a commando raid. U.S. forces used cyber tools during "Operation Absolute Resolve," knocking out power across the capital city. None of this describes or implies a seismic capability. The theory takes a real statement about a real electronic warfare tool used in a real military operation and silently substitutes "earthquake weapon" — a substitution the original statement does not support in any reading. Trump made no statement about causing earthquakes. The June 2026 earthquakes occurred months after the January raid. Treating the "Discombobulator" as a seismic weapon requires ignoring everything Trump actually said about what the weapon did and what operation it was used in.

The underlying physics claim fares no better. HAARP's radio transmissions interact primarily with charged particles in the upper atmosphere. These radio waves do not penetrate deeply into the Earth's crust, nor do they generate the mechanical stress required to influence tectonic activity. The electromagnetic energy transmitted by HAARP is many orders of magnitude smaller than the forces required to trigger a seismic event. Experts in seismology and atmospheric science have weighed in: the consensus is that while HAARP can affect the ionosphere, there is no credible evidence linking its operations to earthquake generation. This is not contested among geoscientists. The theory's assertion of "four anomalies" in the seismic signature that "confirm artificial induction" is unfalsifiable on its face — no specific anomalies are identified, no seismograms are cited, and no mechanism is offered for how radio frequency energy in the ionosphere could lock and then release kilometers of fault at a chosen moment. By contrast, USGS seismologist Paul Earle explained that when earthquakes are this close together in time, it can be difficult to unravel exact magnitudes and locations because the seismograph signals overlap — a straightforward technical observation that conspiracy accounts routinely misread as evidence of fabrication.

The seismic weapon narrative conveniently displaces scrutiny of those domestic governance failures onto a foreign adversary.
