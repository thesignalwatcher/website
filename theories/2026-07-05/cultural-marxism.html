Voice of Reason

The "Cultural Marxism" conspiracy theory holds that a coordinated network of Marxist intellectuals — particularly the Frankfurt School — deliberately seeded progressive ideas into Western universities, media, and culture in order to destroy civilization from within, and that social movements like feminism and LGBTQ+ rights are instruments of that subversion rather than products of genuine grievance.

The core empirical problem with this theory is that it fundamentally misrepresents what the Frankfurt School actually was and what it actually did. The Frankfurt School was a label applied to a disparate intellectual tradition that emerged from the Institute of Social Research, established in Frankfurt in 1923 by a set of Marxist intellectuals seeking to marry Marxism with psychoanalysis and classical sociology. It became famous for its development of "critical theory," which insisted that sociology should not simply describe society but seek also to transform it. The school's major figures — including Max Horkheimer, Theodor Adorno, Herbert Marcuse — were Jewish and had to flee to the United States. These were academics in exile from fascism, not masterminds of a civilizational demolition project. What did they actually believe? Their central argument was paradoxical: the Enlightenment's promise of liberation through reason had curdled into a new form of domination. Instrumental reason — the kind that calculates means to ends without asking about values — had come to dominate nature, society, and the human mind itself. Horkheimer and Adorno went so far in their support of the Enlightenment as to maintain that freedom in society is inseparable from Enlightenment thinking. Adorno, the supposed cultural infiltrator, was so reluctant to translate theory into political action that the Socialist German Students' Union disrupted his lectures for his lack of political engagement; when students occupied a room in January 1969, Adorno called the police to remove them. The conspiracy's alleged chief strategist was in practice opposed to radical student activism — a detail that exposes the gap between the caricature and the historical record.

Scholarly analysis of the conspiracy theory has concluded that it has no basis in fact. The contemporary version of the conspiracy theory originated in the United States during the 1990s and takes inspiration from the Nazi propaganda term "Cultural Bolshevism." Archival research, most recently synthesized in historian A.J.A. Woods's book *The Cultural Marxism Conspiracy* (2026), traces its genealogy to the schisms of the late 1960s left, specifically the Lyndon LaRouche cult. In the 1960s, LaRouche saw the emerging New Left and student protest movements as agents of the CIA — "zombie brainwashers" preparing the way for a fascist takeover by dismembering Western culture. A close associate, Michael Minnicino, condemned the century-long "conspiracy to popularise theories that were specifically designed to weaken the soul of Judaeo-Christian civilisation," insisting that "the single, most important organisational component of this conspiracy was the Frankfurt School." This fringe framework was then adopted by paleoconservative operatives like William Lind and the Free Congress Foundation, who repackaged it for mainstream conservative audiences in the 1990s. Minnicino himself later publicly repudiated the work, admitting that his research on the Frankfurt School had been "hopelessly deformed by self-censorship and the desire to in some way support LaRouche's crack-brained worldview." The foundational text of the modern conspiracy theory was thus disowned by one of its own primary authors.

The claim that feminism and the LGBTQ+ movement are Marxist fronts rather than organic responses to genuine social conditions is empirically falsified by the historical record of both movements. There were few organizations or resources for LGBTQ+ people before the scientific and political revolutions of the 18th and 19th centuries; gradually, the growth of public media and ideals of human rights drew together activists, who drew courage from sympathetic medical studies, banned literature, and emerging sex research. By the 20th century, a movement in recognition of gays and lesbians was underway, abetted by the social climate of feminism and new anthropologies of difference. The earliest known LGBTQ rights organization, the Scientific-Humanitarian Committee, was founded in Berlin in 1897 — decades before the Frankfurt School published a word. The histories of feminism and LGBT politics have been intertwined since at least the 1960s, when both emerged as radical, broadly based social movements amidst the rapid cultural changes of the sexual revolution. They often influenced each other and both radicalised earlier approaches centred on formal legal equality, more fundamentally questioning ingrained social norms. These movements have internal disagreements, rival factions, competing strategies, and distinct histories that no coordinating mastermind could plausibly have engineered or maintained. The conspiracy theory's central reasoning failure is confusing correlation with coordination: the fact that progressive social changes occurred roughly simultaneously across the postwar West is treated as proof of orchestration, when the parsimonious explanation is that industrialization, urbanization, mass media, and the post-WWII expansion of rights discourse created shared conditions for social movements across many countries simultaneously.

The theory does tap into a real and defensible concern: that universities and media institutions carry their own cultural assumptions, that those assumptions have shifted since the 1960s, and that this shift is worth debating openly. Those are legitimate observations, and they deserve substantive engagement. When critics cast disparate progressive ideas as a unified conspiracy, it simplifies heterogeneous movements into a monolithic enemy and can encourage partisan backlash — foreclosing the very debate that genuine institutional critique requires. But the conspiracy version does something more dangerous: it renders the concern permanently unfalsifiable by defining any counterevidence as further proof of the conspiracy's reach. More concretely, the theory has demonstrably incited lethal violence. Anders Behring Breivik, a Norwegian neo-Nazi and domestic terrorist, perpetrated the 2011 Norway attacks; a believer in the Great Replacement conspiracy theory, he sought to combat perceived "Cultural Marxism" by detonating a car bomb at the executive government quarter in Oslo, then carrying out a mass shooting at a Labour Party youth camp, killing 77 people and injuring over 323. In the hours before commencing the attack, Breivik uploaded his 1,518-page manifesto to the internet. It has since become, in the words of extremism researcher J.M. Berger, "the baton in a relay race of extremists, passed from one terrorist murderer to the next through online communities." The conspiracy theory version is not only false, but there is no intellectual movement by that name — only a manufactured genealogy of harm, built on misrepresented scholarship, laundered through the far-right fringe, and weaponized against people whose actual offense is existing in public life.
