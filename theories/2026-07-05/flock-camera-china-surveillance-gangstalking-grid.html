Voice of Reason

This theory claims that Flock Safety's automated license plate reader cameras, deployed in thousands of American municipalities, are secretly operated as nodes in a Chinese government surveillance grid and serve as the backbone of a coordinated "gangstalking" infrastructure targeting individuals who have become aware of the operation. Every factual premise supporting this claim fails under scrutiny, while the genuine civil liberties concerns the theory distorts are well-documented through legitimate channels.

Begin with the corporate record. Flock Group Inc., doing business as Flock Safety, is an American manufacturer and operator of security hardware and software, particularly automated license plate recognition systems. Founded in 2017, it operates under contract with law enforcement agencies, neighborhood associations, and private property owners. The company was founded by CEO Garrett Langley after he was the victim of a property crime in Atlanta, when police were unable to help him track down suspects because of a lack of evidence. The investor base is entirely traceable and wholly American: Flock Safety's backers include Andreessen Horowitz, Kleiner Perkins, Tiger Global, and Matrix Partners. Bedrock, located in San Francisco, Andreessen Horowitz, located in New York City, and Y Combinator, located in Mountain View, are among its institutional investors. No Chinese state-linked entity, no CCP-affiliated fund, and no Chinese investor of any kind appears anywhere in the public record of Flock's nine funding rounds, its cap table, or its board of directors. The theory asserts the opposite without producing a single name, document, or transaction to support it. That is not a minor evidentiary gap — it is the complete absence of a mechanism. A claim that a billion-dollar American company incorporated in Georgia, audited by American investors, serving American federal and state law enforcement, and reportedly in contention for FBI contracts, is secretly a Chinese intelligence asset requires more than assertion; it requires proof that does not exist.

The specific sub-claims collapse individually. The theory alleges that license plate data is "transmitted to Chinese intelligence." Flock's data architecture, as documented by critics, runs on American cloud infrastructure and routes data to American law enforcement agencies — the actual documented controversy is that it routes too much data to too many American agencies, not foreign ones. Flock sells their cloud-connected cameras to police departments and private customers across the nation, pulls license plate readings into their own servers, and allows police to do nationwide searches of the resulting database. The theory's claim about "biometric" collection is similarly unfounded: unlike many surveillance companies, Flock does not use facial recognition and limits data collection to vehicle information. The "gangstalking infrastructure" claim is the most detached from reality. Gangstalking refers to a persecutory belief system wherein sufferers believe they are being followed and harassed by a vast coordinated network; those affected frequently receive psychiatric diagnoses, though they reject this formulation, and self-identify as targeted individuals. A study determined that all 128 cases of self-reported gangstalking in the sample were "highly likely to have been delusional," as defined by the DSM-V. It is common for such plaintiffs to have paranoid beliefs specifically about GPS, telecommunications, radio frequencies, artificial intelligence, and hacking. Surveillance cameras are simply the current technological vessel into which a pre-existing persecutory framework is poured; the theory provides no mechanism, no documented target, and no evidence that any Flock system has ever been used for the coordinated harassment it describes.

There is a legitimate kernel here, and it matters precisely because the conspiracy theory crowds it out. The Electronic Frontier Foundation conducted investigations into Flock Safety's ALPR network, revealing a system susceptible to serious abuses, sparking state and federal investigations and landmark litigation, and documenting how Flock's infrastructure allowed law enforcement to track protesters and surveil women seeking reproductive healthcare. Flock's surveillance technology is criticized for its broadening of public surveillance and a chilling effect on civil liberties, as described by the EFF and the ACLU. A single Flock query can probe over 6,800 networks and access more than 83,000 cameras spanning nearly the entire country, with minimal oversight. These are documented harms traceable to identifiable American actors — local police departments, federal immigration enforcement, and Flock itself — operating under American law with insufficient legislative guardrails. That is a real and serious problem. The conspiracy theory transforms it into something unfalsifiable and xenophobic, substituting "CCP" for actual accountability, which means energy spent chasing a fictional Chinese plot is energy not spent on the real legislative and judicial remedies that civil liberties organizations are actively and successfully pursuing. The backlash to Flock's technology has already led to new state laws designed to protect citizens against alleged abuses of its ALPR systems. The path to addressing the genuine concern runs through democratic institutions, not through a framework that treats every camera as an enemy node and every awareness of the issue as a sign one is being personally targeted.

The harm the theory causes is therefore twofold. It misdirects attention from a real, addressable domestic surveillance problem toward an evidence-free foreign threat narrative that cannot be falsified by any data — because any absence of evidence is reinterpreted as proof of the conspiracy's sophistication. And for the subset of people who genuinely believe they are being personally tracked and harassed through this system, T.I.s describe considerable suffering from ongoing concerns about being harassed, physical symptoms, and the significant social stigma of sharing their claims with family and mental health professionals who dismiss them. Embedding those individuals more deeply in an online community that validates and elaborates their persecution framework rather than connecting them with clinical support does them measurable harm. The Flock camera is a real and controversial technology with real and contested impacts. It is not a Chinese weapon. Those are not the same thing, and conflating them serves no one's interests except those who prefer an unfalsifiable narrative to an actionable one.
