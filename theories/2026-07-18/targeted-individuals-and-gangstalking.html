Voice of Reason

The "targeted individual" and gangstalking belief system holds that government agencies, working in concert with organized criminal networks, use directed energy weapons, covert surveillance, implanted microchips, and 5G infrastructure to systematically harass, surveil, and destroy ordinary civilians who have been placed on secret targeting lists — all under a comprehensive, multi-agency cover-up. The theory incorporates real phenomena — Havana Syndrome, historical government surveillance abuses, and the existence of directed energy research — and uses them as scaffolding for a construction that the evidence does not support.

Start with what the psychiatric and legal records actually show. A study examining 128 cases of self-reported gangstalking found all of them "highly likely to have been delusional," as defined by the DSM-5. Research published in *Culture, Medicine and Psychiatry* analyzed the belief system and concluded that gangstalking is "a Western cultural notion which refers to systematic harassment, surveillance, and torture from unseen or covert assailants or networks" and is "not a 'real phenomenon' compared with genuine stalking." Clinically, gangstalking is most often diagnosed as paranoid schizophrenia. The courts provide a further reality check. A review of U.S. federal cases from 2018 to 2023 that included the term "gang stalking" found that judges assessed 80 such cases, and in 90 percent of them plaintiffs represented themselves — a likely indicator that attorneys declined to take their cases. Reviewers found that 87.5% of cases had portions dismissed for procedural or meritless claims. No available source identifies a decided civil case where a court accepted "gangstalking" as an independent legal theory and awarded damages on that basis alone. The narrative that the entire legal system is co-opted into silence confronts a simple arithmetic problem: the theory requires the coordinated, leak-proof complicity of judges, clerks, attorneys, opposing counsel, and federal appellate panels — a secret-keeping apparatus of implausible scale with zero documentary trail.

The theory's use of Havana Syndrome as proof that the government already deploys directed energy weapons on civilians requires careful, evidence-led reading of what that story actually is. The AHI cases have been investigated by organizations including the State Department, the FBI's Behavioral Analysis Unit, the CIA, the NIH, the NASEM, and seven intelligence agencies under the ODNI. Official investigations have provided various theories on the cause, including directed-energy weapons, psychological and social causes, and toxic chemicals, but there is no consensus. Critically, as of a January 2025 National Intelligence Council report, five IC components placed emphasis on "sensitive intelligence reporting continuing to point away from foreign involvement in AHIs." Even where directed energy effects on the human body are under active research — a Norwegian government scientist in 2024 built a device capable of emitting powerful pulses of microwave energy and tested it on himself, with results that complicated the picture — those aware of that test say it does not prove AHIs are the work of a foreign adversary wielding a secret weapon. The Havana Syndrome debate concerns possible attacks on named, security-cleared government officers at specific diplomatic postings — not the covert, city-wide infrastructure targeting of anonymous civilians. Invoking it to validate the gangstalking framework is a category error: it transfers a contested, still-unresolved intelligence dispute into an entirely different context for which no comparable evidence exists. As for the claim that 5G infrastructure forms the backbone of a targeting system, the frequencies used in 5G cannot penetrate the body beyond a few millimeters of skin, and a study from Germany's Constructor University found that exposure to 5G frequencies, even at ten times recommended limits, produced no changes in gene expression or methylation profiles in human cells. The claim about non-consensual implanted microchips similarly lacks any documented forensic evidence — no explanted device, no credible imaging study, no corroborating medical record — despite the existence of thousands of claimants who have actively sought such evidence.

There is a genuine kernel of concern here that the theory exploits. The U.S. government has a documented history of non-consensual experimentation and covert surveillance programs, from COINTELPRO to MKUltra, and real stalking — including coordinated harassment by multiple people — does occur and is prosecutable. Federal juries have convicted individuals of interstate stalking and cyberstalking involving conspiratorial harassment, demonstrating that courts do prosecute coordinated harassment when prosecutors can identify defendants, overt acts, and statutory violations. Real grievances about surveillance, institutional distrust, and the vulnerability of people without institutional power are legitimate starting points. But the gangstalking framework transforms those grievances into an unfalsifiable system: every absence of evidence becomes evidence of cover-up, every skeptic becomes a plant, and every psychiatric evaluation becomes a tactic of the very persecution being alleged. When targeted individuals are labeled mentally ill online or receive clinical diagnoses, TIs tend to reject this by arguing that their appearance of mental illness is merely a goal of their gangstalkers. This self-sealing logic makes the theory immune to correction by design.

The documented harms are substantial and move in two directions. Believers themselves suffer gravely: those who hold the belief report worse depression, post-traumatic symptoms, suicidal ideation, and longer-lasting distress than those experiencing ordinary stalking. Targeted Individuals communities often lack clinical support, increasing feelings of isolation and misunderstanding. The belief system also generates outward violence. TIs have committed at least four mass shootings or acts of violence in the United States since 2013, with the most notable being the 2013 Navy Yard shooting which killed 12 people. In June 2024, Michael William Nash shot and injured nine people at a Michigan splash pad and was later found dead from an apparent self-inflicted wound; his mother informed law enforcement that Nash "had been suffering with paranoia that the government was watching him." A peer-reviewed phenomenology study found that 32% of self-reporting gangstalking sufferers described experiences in the category of "determined to fight back" and 16% expressed development of violent tendencies. Online communities where TI beliefs circulate overlap extensively with subreddits related to conspiracy theories and mental health diagnoses including bipolar disorder and schizophrenia — suggesting that the internet infrastructure maintaining this belief system is functioning as a vector of harm amplification, not truth-telling. The theory does not lead people toward safety or justice. It leads them away from care and, in the most tragic cases, toward violence against strangers they have recast as unseen persecutors.
