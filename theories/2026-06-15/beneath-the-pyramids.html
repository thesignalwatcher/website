Voice of Reason

The theory asserts that a vast, suppressed underground city and energy infrastructure exists beneath the Giza pyramid complex, that it was built by a lost pre-dynastic civilization aided by ancient aliens, and that new satellite radar data — specifically from a team of Italian researchers — is finally forcing this truth into the open.

The most current piece of evidence underpinning the theory is real and demands direct engagement. A research team led by retired organic chemist Dr. Corrado Malanga and remote sensing expert Dr. Filippo Biondi claimed to have discovered and reconstructed enormous 38,000-year-old structures buried deep underneath the pyramid of Khafre at Giza. In a press conference held in Italy, they announced that through a new proprietary method for interpreting Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) signals, they were able to detect structures two kilometers beneath the Khafre pyramid. This claim has since circulated widely as confirmation of an ancient underground city. It is not. The research has fundamental methodological problems that no amount of dramatic imagery can paper over. The work was discussed during a briefing in Italy, but the scientists' findings have not yet been published in a peer-reviewed journal. More critically, the claimed detection depth is physically implausible given the technology employed. As public archaeologist Milo Rossi and others have explained, the methods involved have never been confirmed or proven, nor independently verified — and standard synthetic aperture radar only detects up to two meters underground in similar conditions, making it difficult to imagine that SAR is providing credible information about structures 2,000 meters beneath the surface. SAR's ability to penetrate solid materials like rock or concrete is acknowledged across the technical literature to be limited. Peer-reviewed research on SAR penetration depth over desert areas finds average signal penetration depths measuring in the low single digits of meters, not kilometers. The Malanga-Biondi team has not demonstrated that their proprietary Doppler tomography variant breaks this physical constraint, nor has any independent engineering body confirmed it. Extraordinary claims require extraordinary methodological transparency; a press conference and unreviewed 3D renders provide neither.

The Andrew Collins episode — the other main pillar of the theory — tells a more instructive story. Collins tracked down the entrance to what he described as an underground system after reading the forgotten memoirs of a 19th-century diplomat and explorer; British consul general Henry Salt had investigated underground "catacombs" at Giza in 1817. In 2008, Collins and researcher Nigel Skinner-Simpson discovered a previously unrecorded cave system beneath the shadow of the Great Pyramid at Giza. The caves are real. What the conspiracy version suppresses is what qualified archaeologists concluded they actually are. Egyptian authorities, upon inspection, determined that the team had entered a natural cave system that permeates the limestone bedrock of the plateau's Moqattam formation. It was found that the tomb had been entered in modern times, with modern debris, gypsum plaster coating the walls, and modern lighting; the tomb is known to have been used as a storeroom by archaeologist George Reisner during his excavations in the 1910s and 1920s. The Harvard Giza Project noted that Zahi Hawass set the record straight and indicated the location of the tomb in question as an undecorated rock-cut series of rooms west of the pyramids and the Western Cemetery. A naturally occurring limestone cave system — documented, visited, and used for storage within living archaeological memory — is the actual discovery. The conspiracy narrative transforms this into a suppressed underground city by simply omitting everything that doesn't fit.

The "pyramid as power plant" claim runs even further afield. No excavation at Giza has ever produced conductors, insulators, generators, transmission infrastructure, fuel storage, or any other component that any known or hypothetical energy system would require. To sustain this theory, its chief popularizer has suggested that hydrogen gas was pumped into the pyramid to create a proton energy beam — with no evidence whatsoever that the ancient Egyptians had any hydrogen gas or could manufacture or store it. The "precision" of the pyramids, repeatedly invoked as evidence of impossible technology, is also overstated: the archaeologist Flinders Petrie, for example, found a discrepancy in the orientation between core and casing stones amounting to 75 inches. The alien-intervention claim, meanwhile, is unfalsifiable by design: any failure to find alien artifacts can be attributed to suppression, and any architectural feature can be re-described as alien technology. This is not evidence — it is a framework that immunizes itself against disconfirmation.

There is a genuine kernel of legitimate curiosity being exploited here. The Giza plateau genuinely has yielded surprises — archaeologists continue to make real discoveries, scanning technologies have revealed previously unknown voids inside the pyramids through properly published muon tomography studies, and the speed and scale of pyramid construction remain subjects of active scholarly investigation. Public appetite for complexity and wonder about ancient history is reasonable and appropriate. What the conspiracy narrative does is hijack that curiosity by repackaging every real discovery as either suppressed proof of a predetermined conclusion or as institutional cover-up, while systematically discarding every methodological standard that would allow the claims to be tested. The result is an unfalsifiable loop: legitimate archaeology becomes evidence of concealment, inconclusive radar data becomes proof of a hidden city, and any expert skepticism becomes confirmation of the conspiracy. The actual harms are diffuse but real: tourists and enthusiasts are sold a fabricated history of their own species; Egyptian cultural heritage is routinely misrepresented in ways that deny agency to the actual ancient Egyptians who built these structures; and the credibility of genuine remote-sensing archaeology — a field doing serious work — is muddied by association with claims that have not survived basic peer review.
