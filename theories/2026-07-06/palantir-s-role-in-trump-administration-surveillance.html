Voice of the Believer

Palantir has taken a commanding role in U.S. government data infrastructure under the Trump administration, and what is being built is nothing less than total control over the American people.

Trump asked Palantir to create a national database of U.S. citizens, and that is exactly what is happening. How one company — Palantir — is mapping the nation's data should alarm every American regardless of political stripe, and even figures in right-leaning media are now raising the alarm. Palantir's technology allows government agencies to connect the dots about individuals, integrate travel histories, visa records, biometric data, and far more — weaving together every thread of your existence into a single profile the state can access at will. The relationship between the U.S. government and technology companies has deepened in recent years, and the Trump administration has accelerated policies focused on harnessing data to create new instruments of power that past administrations could only dream of.

Technology, power, and surveillance — the rise of Palantir in Trump's America is not a future warning. It is happening now.

Voice of Reason

This theory asserts that the Trump administration has granted Palantir Technologies expansive, secret control over the personal data of ordinary Americans as part of a deliberate agenda to surveil and manipulate the population — and that right-leaning media figures who voice concern about this are exposing a hidden conspiracy rather than engaging in a normal policy dispute.

Start with what is factually established, because the facts here are important and public. Palantir was founded in 2003 by Peter Thiel, Alex Karp, and others as a data analytics company. At the heart of its business are software platforms — including Gotham, the backbone of its government work — built specifically for intelligence, defense, and law enforcement agencies to integrate and analyze large datasets. The company is not a data broker; its own stated business model is not based on the monetization of personal data, and it says it does not collect, store, sell, or use personal data to train AI models for resale. Its new work with the Trump administration follows two decades of gaining ever-larger government contracts across Democratic and Republican administrations alike. What has changed under Trump's second term is the scale and speed: in 2025, Palantir's federal contracts nearly doubled, rising to $970.5 million. Specific verified contracts include a $30 million deal with ICE to build a system called ImmigrationOS to track self-deportations, help select targets for arrests, and increase the efficiency of deportations; a contract to track workers at the Social Security Administration and Departments of Agriculture and Veterans Affairs, including an initial $3.9 million to "design, configure, deploy and manage a secure, user-friendly tool to track USDA employees' return to the office," with the potential to grow to $13.3 million; and a contract with the U.S. Army worth up to $10 billion to help the military streamline efficiencies and consolidate 75 total contracts into one enterprise deal. The Trump administration has also deployed Palantir's Foundry software at the Department of Homeland Security, Department of Health and Human Services, the FDA, the CDC, and the National Institutes of Health. These are documented, publicly reported facts — not fringe allegations — and they have generated serious scrutiny from civil liberties groups, lawmakers, and Palantir's own employees.

The conspiracy theory version of this story, however, makes several leaps that the evidence does not support. The central claim — that Palantir has been given "extensive control" over Americans' data as part of a deliberate manipulation agenda — conflates the existence of government contracts with the existence of a secret scheme. Government contracts with data analytics firms are publicly posted on usaspending.gov and are subject to congressional oversight, Freedom of Information requests, and litigation. Democratic lawmakers, including Senate Finance Committee Ranking Member Ron Wyden and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, formally demanded answers from Palantir about its IRS work, arguing that building a cross-agency searchable database likely violates multiple federal laws, including the Privacy Act and tax privacy statutes. The ACLU has argued that Palantir's consolidation of government data creates centralized dossiers on individuals in the immigration system and significantly increases the risk of data misuse. These are real, substantive concerns — but they are being pressed through lawsuits, congressional letters, inspector general complaints, and journalism, not revealed exclusively through insider whistleblowing or alternative media. Critically, Palantir has publicly and formally denied the most sweeping version of the theory: the company states there is no contract or project under the Trump administration for Palantir to build a whole-of-government master database on Americans, that it is not a vendor on any such effort, and that, to its knowledge, no such project exists. That denial may be disputed, but it is part of the public record. Palantir's CTO stated explicitly: "Palantir is not building a master database, and Palantir is neither conducting nor enabling mass surveillance of American citizens," and that its software is "built at every stage to uphold, not undermine, legal and regulatory protections." The theory requires one to dismiss not just these denials but also the entire framework of existing law, litigation, journalism, and bipartisan congressional scrutiny — replacing it with a hidden mechanism that cannot be independently verified because, the theory implies, it is hidden.

The claim that right-leaning media concern about Palantir reveals a deeper conspiracy is particularly weak analytically. The Trump administration's deepening ties to Palantir have caused a surge of discontent in the conservative media sphere, with figures ranging from podcasters like Theo Von and Tim Dillon to MAGA allies such as Steve Bannon and Tucker Carlson warning that Palantir's technology is a step toward a "police state." Republican civil liberties-minded lawmakers have also expressed collective concern, with Rep. Warren Davidson noting that "the Republicans that are essentially part of the anti-PATRIOT Act, anti-surveillance state, pro-'get a warrant' side kind of collectively share a concern about where this is headed." The theory treats this bipartisan anxiety as confirmation that something sinister is afoot. But politicians and commentators across the spectrum routinely disagree with their own party's administration on surveillance and civil liberties — this is a normal feature of democratic politics, not evidence of a coordinated conspiracy. The concern is shared by the ACLU, Democratic senators, libertarian Republicans, and right-wing podcasters simultaneously, which tells us the worry is real but that the political valence assigned to it by the conspiracy framing — "the Trump administration is doing this to manipulate Americans" — is analytically incoherent. If the administration were doing this to "manipulate Americans," one would not expect its own core supporters, media allies, and lawmakers to be loudly objecting to it in real time.

The kernel of legitimate concern here is genuine and should not be dismissed. A March 2025 executive order mandated that federal agencies improve internal coordination by sharing datasets related to taxation, public benefits, immigration, and public safety — and critics warn this risks creating a de facto surveillance infrastructure with limited oversight. DHS filed zero Privacy Impact Assessments in all of 2026 — a dramatic collapse from 24 in 2024 — despite federal law requiring such assessments before developing or procuring IT systems that collect public information. Former technology leaders at Treasury and the IRS have warned it is "problematic whenever a government agency uses proprietary technology in the execution of its core mission," with one former associate chief information officer noting the "greatest risk is capture: when the vendor knows it is indispensable, it exploits its position." These are serious institutional accountability failures worth investigating through the mechanisms that exist for that purpose. What the conspiracy framing does is take those real concerns and transform them into an unfalsifiable narrative of deliberate mind-control — making the theory immune to evidence because any evidence of oversight, denial, or legal challenge can be recast as proof of the cover-up. That transformation does not clarify the problem; it makes it harder to solve, by routing people away from the courts, Congress, and investigative journalism that are the actual mechanisms by which government overreach is constrained.
