Voice of the Believer

Organized, paid operatives systematically collect absentee and mail-in ballots from nursing homes and vulnerable communities, manipulating those ballots to manufacture votes for Democrats. The practice operates as a coordinated electoral fraud machine, exploiting legal gray zones in states where third-party ballot collection is permitted. The result is that Democratic victories in competitive races are not earned at the ballot box but manufactured through systemic ballot manipulation.

Ballot harvesting is a coordinated operation in which paid political operatives — known in some states as 'boleteros' in Florida and operating similarly in Texas — canvass nursing homes, low-income neighborhoods, and other vulnerable communities to collect completed absentee and mail-in ballots. These operatives do not merely deliver blank ballots; they target residents who may be confused, coerced, or otherwise unable to protect the integrity of their own vote. The ballots are then manipulated before submission, replacing voter intent with the preferences of the harvesting organization.

The operation functions as a vote-manufacturing infrastructure for the Democratic Party. Because ballot harvesting is legal in a majority of states, operatives work openly, using the veneer of legality to conduct what is in practice organized electoral fraud. The legal permission structure is itself a vulnerability that bad actors exploit at scale, turning a practice nominally designed to aid voter participation into a mechanism for generating fraudulent Democratic votes.

Reports from Minneapolis illustrate the scope of the problem: investigators looked into accounts of supporters of Rep. Ilhan Omar engaging in ballot harvesting, with allegations that ballots were collected and handled in ways that compromised their integrity. Cynthia Harris, a former Orange County Commission candidate, described a ballot harvester arriving at her home — a personal account of how these operatives insert themselves directly into the voting process. These are not isolated incidents but symptoms of a nationwide, organized effort to tilt elections.

Voice of Reason

The theory under examination holds that third-party ballot collection — commonly called ballot harvesting — is not merely a civic practice but the operational infrastructure of a coordinated Democratic Party fraud scheme, through which paid operatives manipulate absentee ballots collected from nursing homes and vulnerable communities to manufacture Democratic victories. The evidence does not support this.

Ballot harvesting refers to the practice of collecting completed absentee ballots from voters and delivering them to polling places or election offices. These efforts often focus on helping elderly voters or people who live in remote areas ensure their ballot gets in on time. As of recent years, 18 states allowed anyone chosen by the voter to return a ballot on the voter's behalf, and 16 additional states allowed anyone with certain qualifying relationships to the voter to do so. The fraud narrative treats this legal infrastructure as self-evidently corrupt, but that move requires proof, not assertion. The actual empirical record on scale of fraud is devastating to the theory: cases of mail voting fraud account for only 0.000043% of total mail ballots cast, or about four cases out of every ten million mail votes. A study published by the American Statistical Association found no evidence that voting by mail increases the risk of voter fraud overall, with states conducting elections largely by mail reporting fraud rates similar to other states. The Heritage Foundation's own database — maintained by an organization strongly opposed to ballot harvesting — identifies just 1,285 cases of potential voter fraud from hundreds of millions of ballots cast over twenty years, and only 204 of those involved absentee or vote-by-mail ballots. No database, government audit, or peer-reviewed study has produced evidence of a coordinated, partisan, large-scale ballot manipulation operation operating anywhere in the country.

The specific allegations the theory recycles are weaker still. The Minneapolis case stems from a 2020 Project Veritas video purporting to connect Representative Ilhan Omar to illegal ballot collection. The man shown in the video, Liban Mohamed, is the brother of a Minneapolis city council candidate, Jamal Osman; a spokesperson for Omar clarified that Mohamed does not work for the congresswoman. Moreover, in Minnesota, ballot harvesting is legal. The Minneapolis Police Department said it would evaluate the allegations, but there have been no formal accusations or legal findings linking Omar or her campaign to illegal activities. The theory uses the opening of an evaluation as though it were a finding of guilt — a fundamental inversion of evidentiary logic. As for the broader claim that harvesting is a Democratic-party operation, the most consequential, fully documented case of real ballot fraud involving third-party collection in recent American history ran in precisely the opposite direction: Leslie McCrae Dowless was a Republican political operative in North Carolina at the center of a fraud investigation following the 2018 congressional district election. Testimony established that Dowless and his associates collected ballots from voters, kept them at his home or office for days, and that operatives filled in votes on parts or all of some ballots to favor Republican candidates. North Carolina's election commission determined the doubts surrounding the integrity of the election were sufficiently serious that results should be invalidated and a new election held. This case — in which ballot manipulation actually occurred, was prosecuted, and invalidated an election — benefited a Republican candidate, not a Democrat. The conspiracy theory erases this inconvenient example entirely.

There is a genuine concern that the theory exploits without honestly engaging: absentee ballot systems do carry marginally higher theoretical vulnerability to third-party interference than in-person voting, and evidence shows that when fraud does occur, election administrators identify it and take action, correcting election returns and prosecuting those responsible. Legitimate policy debate exists about how tightly to regulate who may return a ballot on a voter's behalf. Critics reasonably fear that in states without restrictions on who can return a ballot on someone else's behalf, a third party could take advantage of the system by tampering with or discarding the ballots. These are real governance questions. But the conspiracy theory hijacks that concern and converts it into an unfalsifiable narrative: every Democratic win in a state that permits ballot collection becomes evidence of fraud, every investigation that fails to find wrongdoing becomes a cover-up, and every individual account of a harvester knocking on a door becomes proof of an organized manufacturing operation. No evidence could, under this framing, disprove the theory — which is the hallmark of propaganda, not analysis.

The concrete harm this narrative causes is measurable. One Arizona election official noted that, aside from individual isolated instances she could count on one hand with fingers to spare, she had seen no credible evidence of any organized effort of unauthorized persons delivering ballots, let alone widespread invalid votes being cast — and that real harm had been caused by unsupported allegations, including new laws put in place in response to alleged voter fraud that limit voter access. By casting legal ballot assistance programs as partisan fraud machinery, the narrative places legitimate voter-assistance infrastructure under suspicion, discourages voters in remote, elderly, or disabled communities from using legal collection services, and provides political cover for legislation that restricts access to the ballot rather than protecting its integrity.
