Voice of Reason

The theory being addressed holds that Illuminati: New World Order, a collectible card game released in 1994, contains cards that predicted specific future events — including the September 11 attacks and Donald Trump's rise to the presidency — because the game's designers were connected to, or briefed by, an actual secret global elite. The eye-in-the-pyramid imagery throughout the game is read as a genuine emblem of this cabal, and the game is treated as a kind of prophetic disclosure embedded in plain sight.

The factual record dismantles every link in this chain. INWO was released in 1994 by Steve Jackson Games, based on the company's earlier board game Illuminati, which was itself inspired by the 1975 satirical novel The Illuminatus! Trilogy by Robert Anton Wilson and Robert Shea. The game cast players as shadowy organizations — the Bavarian Illuminati, UFOs, Servants of Cthulhu — competing for world domination, and co-author Robert Shea even quipped in the rulebook, "Maybe the Illuminati are behind this game… they are, by definition, behind everything." This is not a secret leaking through; it is a satirical game that was explicitly designed to mock and play with conspiracy-theory tropes for entertainment. Steve Jackson intended pure satire, researching conspiracy theories for entertainment value, while illustrator John Grigni cited post-Soviet terrorism anxieties — specifically mentioning Hamas — when explaining the "Terrorist Nuke" card design, not any mystical foresight. The card most cited as a 9/11 "prediction," the Terrorist Nuke card, depicts two buildings resembling the World Trade Center towers with one of the buildings exploding in the middle. The towers were an obvious cultural landmark and had already survived a truck-bomb attack in 1993, one year before the game's release — they were the natural visual shorthand for a domestic terrorist attack in the early 1990s. A game about catastrophic world events that did not include imagery of tall buildings exploding would have been the anomaly. Former employee Loyd Blankenship compared the phenomenon to psychic predictions: make enough vague disaster forecasts and you will inevitably hit something. The game's hundreds of cards depict disasters, political upheavals, pandemics, media manipulation, corrupt politicians, celebrity deaths, financial crashes, and foreign wars. With that volume of thematic material spanning every major category of human catastrophe, post-hoc matches to real events are a mathematical certainty, not evidence of foreknowledge. No one names the cards that did not "predict" anything, which is precisely the selection bias — known in cognitive science as the Texas sharpshooter fallacy — that sustains the theory.

The "Trump prediction" claim rests on a card depicting a generic blonde demagogue addressing a crowd. Cards depicting scenes including a blonde-haired demagogue addressing supporters have drawn fascination from conspiracy theorists, but charismatic populist leaders were a staple of 20th-century political history long before Donald Trump entered politics, and a game about seizing world domination through manipulation of power structures would obviously include such a figure. No card names Trump, predicts his specific electoral college path, or describes the 2016 political environment. The "prediction" is a generic archetype being mapped onto a specific outcome after the fact. This is retrofitting, not prophecy, and it would work equally well for any number of other political figures before or after Trump.

The theory's claims about the eye-in-the-pyramid symbol are also factually inverted. In 1782, the Eye of Providence was adopted as part of the Great Seal of the United States; the All-Seeing Eye was first suggested by a consultant to the first design committee, Pierre Eugene du Simitiere. The idea that this symbol proves Freemasonry played a role in the country's formation is a common one among conspiracy theorists, but the Eye was first used in Masonry fourteen years after the Great Seal was made. The only Mason among the members of the various design committees for the Great Seal was Benjamin Franklin, whose ideas for the seal were not adopted, and various Masonic organizations have explicitly denied any connection to the creation of the Seal. Steve Jackson adopted the symbol because it was the pre-existing cultural shorthand for his game's subject matter — conspiracy and secret power — drawn directly from the Wilson and Shea novels and from centuries of popular iconography. Its presence on the cards signals "we are doing a conspiracy-themed parody," nothing more.

The kernel of genuine concern that this theory taps into is real enough: popular distrust of political institutions, the sense that powerful actors operate without accountability, and the post-9/11 anxiety that authorities withheld information about threats they had detected. Those are legitimate grievances about transparency and institutional failure. Despite online hysteria and claims of supernatural predictions, the game creators intended pure satire, researching conspiracy theories for entertainment value and emphasizing that the cards' accuracy is coincidental rather than prophetic, feeding into our appetite for pattern recognition in chaotic times. The theory converts that legitimate unease into an unfalsifiable narrative: any card that can be stretched to fit a real event is evidence of the conspiracy; any card that cannot is simply ignored; and any denial by the game's creators is itself reframed as cover. There is no outcome that could, even in principle, disprove the theory on its own terms, which is the defining mark of reasoning that has departed from evidence entirely.

The documented harms of this specific theory are relatively limited compared to more virulent conspiracy clusters. It primarily functions as a gateway into broader conspiratorial worldviews, training the pattern-recognition habits — retroactive matching, selective memory, symbolic reading of coincidence — that make people more susceptible to more dangerous theories downstream. It contributes to the generalized belief that major historical events are scripted and that organic political participation is pointless, a belief that benefits those who want citizens disengaged from democratic accountability. The theory also inflates the resale market for out-of-print cards by treating them as artifacts of hidden knowledge, a harmless financial quirk that nonetheless illustrates how misinformation creates its own economic ecosystem. The real cost is epistemic: every person who concludes that a satirical card game from 1994 confirms the existence of a centuries-old secret directorate managing history is a person whose capacity to evaluate actual institutional misconduct — the kind documented by journalists, investigators, and courts — has been displaced by an untestable fantasy.
