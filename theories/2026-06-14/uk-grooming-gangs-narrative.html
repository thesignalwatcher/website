The conspiracy theory asserts that UK authorities orchestrated a deliberate, top-down cover-up of widespread sexual abuse by Muslim men to advance demographic replacement of white Britons, that this abuse is driven by religious ideology targeting non-Muslim girls, and that officials suppress the truth through biased policing while fabricating inquiries designed to obscure these facts.

The evidence directly contradicts these claims. The 2022 Home Office study on group-based child sexual exploitation found that while some localized networks involved men of Pakistani heritage, the majority of contact child sexual abuse offenders in England and Wales are white British men—approximately 85-90% according to Ministry of Justice and National Crime Agency data. Group-based exploitation represents a small fraction of overall child sexual abuse. The Independent Inquiry into Child Sexual Abuse (IICSA), which concluded in 2022 after examining institutions across the UK, documented failures spanning the Catholic Church, the Church of England, local authorities, and other institutions where perpetrators were overwhelmingly white and British. Claims of "a quarter million victims" or "millions raped" are numerically fabricated, extrapolating wildly from localized case numbers. The UK does not collect crime statistics by religion, making claims about "Muslim offender rates" impossible to substantiate from official data—believers are typically misreading ethnicity data (which conflates Hindu, Sikh, and Muslim populations under "Asian") or citing non-peer-reviewed advocacy group estimates. The assertion that cities contain "no-go zones" under sharia law is demonstrably false; Birmingham, Britain's second-largest city, functions under UK law with Muslim residents comprising roughly 30% of the population, not a controlling majority, and non-Muslims live, work, and travel throughout freely. The 2025 statutory inquiry announced by the government follows years of local inquiries and the IICSA process, responding to calls from multiple parties including abuse survivors—characterizing it preemptively as a "whitewash" renders the theory unfalsifiable.

What makes these narratives superficially plausible is that serious institutional failures did occur. Cases in Rotherham, Rochdale, Telford, and other towns revealed that police and social services ignored victims' reports for years, sometimes citing fears of appearing racist or inflaming community tensions. These failures were real, documented in inquiries like the Jay Report (2014), and represent profound betrayals of vulnerable children. Some networks did involve men of Pakistani heritage exploiting white British girls, and cultural factors including misogyny within certain communities merit honest discussion. However, these localized failures—rooted in bureaucratic cowardice, resource constraints, and class-based dismissal of working-class victims—have been weaponized into an unfalsifiable narrative of deliberate religious warfare and demographic conspiracy. The "two-tier policing" claim misrepresents statistical realities: far-right protesters have received significant sentences, but so have Muslim offenders in grooming cases, often receiving among the longest sentences in the criminal justice system for sexual offenses.
