Voice of Reason

The conspiracy theory alleges that Western governments are systematically erasing the words "mother" and "father" from all legislation, birth certificates, and official documents as part of a coordinated campaign to abolish biological parenthood as a legal category.

The claim fundamentally misrepresents both the scope and purpose of the legislative changes it cites. New York's 2022 legislation did not eliminate "mother" and "father" from all state law—it modified parentage and surrogacy statutes specifically to include same-sex couples and surrogacy arrangements by adding inclusive terminology alongside, not replacing, existing language. The bill allows for recognition of intended parents in surrogacy contracts, an arrangement where the person who gives birth may not be the legal parent. Traditional terminology remains throughout family law, vital records, and the overwhelming majority of New York statutes. Wisconsin's proposal similarly addressed narrow parentage provisions related to assisted reproduction, not a wholesale replacement across all state law. France's 2019 administrative change for school enrollment forms—offering "Parent 1" and "Parent 2" as options—did not ban the words "mother" and "father" from schools, legal documents, or public life; parents remain free to identify themselves using traditional terms, and the change aimed to accommodate diverse family structures on optional forms. The U.S. House rules package in 2021 updated the language of House procedural rules to be gender-neutral for the institution's own internal operations—terms like "father, mother, son, daughter" were replaced with "parent, child, sibling" in references to House members' relationships for purposes of congressional proceedings. This had zero effect on federal law, birth certificates, or how families describe themselves.

Birth certificates have not been systematically stripped of parental designations. Some jurisdictions now offer options beyond binary "mother/father" fields to accommodate families formed through adoption, surrogacy, or same-sex partnership, but this is typically additive—providing alternatives rather than prohibitions. The conspiracy theory conflates optional accommodation with mandatory abolition. No coordinated transnational effort exists; the legislative changes cited span different years, different contexts (administrative forms vs. statutes vs. internal procedural rules), and different purposes. The claimed synchronicity is retroactive pattern-matching across unrelated policy debates in different legal systems. The use of "birthing people" or "pregnant people" in some public health contexts reflects medical communication efforts to include transgender men and non-binary people who can become pregnant—it does not replace "mother" in birth certificates, legal parentage, or general usage.

The theory taps into genuine unease about rapid social change and concerns that evolving family structures might diminish recognition of biological motherhood. Some people feel that inclusive language devalues traditional roles. These concerns warrant respectful discussion. But the theory distorts isolated, context-specific updates into fabricated evidence of coordinated abolition, transforming limited administrative accommodations into civilizational erasure, and obscuring the actual continued legal prominence of maternal and paternal categories across all cited jurisdictions.
