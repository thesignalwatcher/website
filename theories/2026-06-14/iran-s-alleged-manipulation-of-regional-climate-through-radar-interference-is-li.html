Voice of Reason

The core assertion is that American radar systems deployed at military bases across the Arab world were intentionally suppressing rainfall to maintain drought conditions, and that Iranian strikes destroying these installations triggered a regional return to normal precipitation, evidenced by overflowing dams and flooding rivers. A secondary claim alleges that Jewish actors coordinated arson in Patagonia to enable cheap land acquisition following President Milei's liberalization of foreign ownership rules.

No such 40-day war between Iran and a US-Israel-Arab coalition occurred in recent history. The timeline described does not correspond to any documented military conflict. While Iran did conduct missile strikes on US bases in Iraq in January 2020 following the Soleimani assassination, this involved neither a coalition of six Arab countries nor a sustained 40-day conflict. More fundamentally, radar systems cannot suppress rainfall. Weather radar detects precipitation by bouncing radio waves off water droplets; it observes weather rather than creates it. Cloud seeding technology exists and has been deployed in limited contexts across the Middle East, but it attempts to enhance rainfall, not suppress it, and operates through aircraft dispersing silver iodide or similar agents—not ground-based radar. The physics simply do not support the mechanism described. Regional precipitation patterns in the Middle East are driven by large-scale atmospheric circulation, topography, and climate oscillations like ENSO. Attributing drought or its end to the presence or absence of military radar installations reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of meteorology. Regarding Patagonia, wildfires did occur in early 2024, and Milei has indeed pursued policies opening Argentine land to foreign investment, but arson investigations are complex and inconclusive, and no credible evidence links Jewish actors specifically to coordinated land-grab schemes through deliberate fire-setting.

The theory resonates because it addresses genuine grievances. The Middle East has experienced severe, prolonged droughts exacerbated by climate change, poor water management, and upstream dam construction by regional powers. Populations have watched water resources dwindle while foreign military installations proliferate on their soil, often with minimal local benefit and considerable sovereignty costs. The suspicion that external powers manipulate regional resources for strategic advantage is not baseless—historical examples of colonial resource extraction and contemporary water politics lend plausibility to the underlying distrust. Similarly, concerns about foreign acquisition of valuable land during disasters have precedent globally, from post-earthquake Haiti to fire-affected California. Milei's libertarian economic policies have generated legitimate anxiety about national asset sales and sovereignty erosion. The theory packages these real concerns into a mechanistic narrative that offers both villain and explanation.

The narrative also carries antisemitic overtones in its Patagonia component, specifically attributing malicious coordination to Jewish actors as an ethnic category rather than particular individuals or entities. This framing risks inciting hostility toward Jewish communities in Argentina and reinforcing classic conspiracy tropes about coordinated schemes for territorial control, patterns with documented histories of fueling violence.
