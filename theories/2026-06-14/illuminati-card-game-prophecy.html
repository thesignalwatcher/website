Voice of Reason

The central assertion is that a collectible card game published in 1995 by Steve Jackson Games contains evidence of foreknowledge of major world events, particularly the September 11 attacks and Donald Trump's presidency, because its designer had access to information from the actual Illuminati organization that orchestrates global events. The game's use of Illuminati iconography is interpreted not as thematic flavor for a satirical game about conspiracy theories, but as proof of insider status.

The evidentiary problems are fundamental. Steve Jackson Games' Illuminati: New World Order is explicitly a parody game based on Robert Anton Wilson and Robert Shea's satirical *Illuminatus!* trilogy from the 1970s. The game contains over 400 cards depicting generic disaster scenarios, political upheavals, and power structures—a shotgun approach that makes retroactive pattern-matching statistically inevitable. The "Terrorist Nuke" card cited as predicting 9/11 shows a generic explosion near buildings, a visual trope common to disaster imagery since King Kong. No card specifically depicts hijacked planes, the World Trade Center's distinctive architecture, or any details that would demonstrate actual foreknowledge rather than coincidental visual similarity. Regarding Trump, no card bears his name, likeness, or describes his specific biography; believers retrofit generic cards about "charismatic leaders" or "populist movements" after the fact. This is classic confirmation bias: scanning hundreds of vague cards for any that could be reinterpreted to fit later events while ignoring the hundreds that predicted nothing. The eye-in-pyramid symbol appears because the game is literally about Illuminati conspiracy theories as its core mechanic—it would be stranger if it didn't use that iconography. The designer has repeatedly stated the obvious: he was making a humorous game about conspiracy culture, not receiving briefings from shadow governments.

The theory resonates because it offers a seductive narrative structure to chaotic events. The September 11 attacks were genuinely shocking and explanations involving intelligence failures feel inadequate compared to the scale of the trauma. Trump's election genuinely surprised political establishments and defied expert predictions, creating real questions about how elites misread public sentiment. The theory converts uncertainty and contingency into a universe where powerful people are always in control, even of catastrophes—a psychologically comforting alternative to accepting that history often unfolds through accidents, miscalculations, and emergent complexity no one fully orchestrates. It also validates a certain intellectual vanity: the believer has "decoded" what ordinary people miss, finding patterns in randomness that prove their superior discernment. The game's very existence as a commercial product creates the illusion of "hiding in plain sight," when it's actually just a niche hobby product doing exactly what it says on the box.
