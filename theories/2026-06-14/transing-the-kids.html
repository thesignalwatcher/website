Voice of Reason

The theory alleges that LGBTQ activists, schools, media companies, and corporations are conducting a coordinated campaign to deliberately convert children to being transgender or gay through exposure to drag performances, Pride imagery in classrooms, and inclusive curricula.

No evidence supports the existence of such a coordinated operation, and the core premise contradicts established developmental science. Sexual orientation and gender identity are not chosen or externally imposed; decades of research confirm they emerge from complex interactions of biological, genetic, and early developmental factors. Conversion therapy—attempts to change sexual orientation or gender identity—has been repeatedly demonstrated to be both ineffective and harmful, which is precisely why it fails when applied in reverse as well. The American Psychological Association, the American Academy of Pediatrics, and every major medical body have concluded that exposure to LGBTQ people or content does not alter a child's sexual orientation or gender identity. Children raised by same-sex parents, for instance, show the same distribution of sexual orientations as children of heterosexual parents. The claim of social contagion rests on misinterpretation: adolescents who come out in peer clusters are exhibiting a well-documented phenomenon where marginalized individuals find safety in numbers and shared language, not "catching" an identity like a virus. The term "Rapid Onset Gender Dysphoria" originated from a single 2018 parent-survey study later corrected for methodological flaws and has never been recognized as a clinical diagnosis by any major medical organization. The rise in adolescent gender-service referrals reflects increased awareness, reduced stigma, and greater access to care—not manufactured identities—much as left-handedness "surged" when teachers stopped forcing children to use their right hands.

The theory weaponizes three legitimate areas of active debate: ongoing scientific discussion about best practices in pediatric gender care, disagreements over age-appropriate curricula and parental notification policies, and questions about how schools balance student privacy with family involvement. The UK's Cass Review identified weak evidence for some pediatric interventions and called for better research, which is a methodological concern, not evidence of a conspiracy. Parents do have standing to debate what is taught and when, and schools have sometimes handled these issues clumsily. But these policy disagreements are framed as proof of malicious intent—an unfalsifiable accusation that recycles the decades-old "homosexual recruitment" libel that falsely painted LGBTQ people as predators. Stripping away the conspiracy superstructure reveals ordinary civic disagreement rendered toxic by defamation.

The theory has driven bomb threats against children's hospitals providing gender care, harassment campaigns against librarians and teachers, legislative bans on evidence-based medical treatment for adolescents, and organized intimidation at public libraries hosting story hours. It has isolated LGBTQ youth, many of whom already face elevated suicide risk, by framing their existence as a threat. The demonization of visibility—Pride flags, inclusive books—has chilled educators and stigmatized families seeking appropriate care for gender-diverse children.
