  • The CIA orchestrated the assassination of President Kennedy.
  • A second shooter was positioned on the grassy knoll in Dealey Plaza and fired at Kennedy, contradicting the single-shooter conclusion.
  • The Warren Commission was a deliberate cover-up, designed to protect those responsible and prevent the true nature of the assassination from being exposed.
  • The Warren Commission's single-bullet theory was fabricated to support a predetermined conclusion of sole-gunman culpability.

  • Oswald was set up as a patsy to absorb sole responsibility for the assassination.

Voice of the Believer

The CIA murdered our President John F. Kennedy, and the cover-up began before the body was cold. Some people believe Lee Harvey Oswald acted alone — that a bitter, unstable ex-Marine with a mail-order rifle somehow pulled off the crime of the century without help, without handlers, without a single loose thread. That story was always meant for people who don't ask follow-up questions. Lee Harvey Oswald was a pawn in a larger conspiracy to assassinate JFK, and he told you so himself, standing in that Dallas corridor: "I am only a patsy." There are many conspiracy theories surrounding the assassination, yes — but the throughline connecting the most credible of them runs straight through Langley. Lee Harvey Oswald's intelligence connections — his defection to the Soviet Union, his fair-play-for-Cuba activities, his inexplicable re-entry into the United States without incident — do not describe a lone wolf. They describe an asset being positioned.

The Warren Commission and the JFK Assassination Records exist in permanent tension with each other for a reason. The Commission was handed its conclusion before it heard its first witness. The single-bullet theory, the magic bullet that changed direction mid-air and somehow preserved itself in near-pristine condition, was not forensic science — it was narrative engineering. The most plausible explanations of who killed President Kennedy all require more than one shooter, and the acoustic and eyewitness evidence from Dealey Plaza points to the grassy knoll with consistency the official story cannot absorb. Lee Harvey Oswald was involved in the assassination, almost certainly — but as a decoy, a face to point at, not as a trigger man operating alone from a sixth-floor window.

Then Jack Ruby walks into Dallas Police Headquarters — walks in, past officers, with a weapon — and silences the one man who could have talked. Ruby's organized crime ties, his documented access, his sudden passionate grief over a president he barely mentioned before that weekend: intelligence agencies were involved in the assassinations of figures threatening the national security state, and Kennedy had made enemies of precisely that apparatus by threatening to dismantle the CIA after the Bay of Pigs disaster. The theories may seem wild, but with so much evidence, folks are still asking — who really killed JFK? The answer has always been hiding in plain sight.

Voice of Reason

The assassination of John F. Kennedy was genuinely chaotic, poorly secured, and followed by an investigation many historians still consider incomplete. Jack Ruby did have organized-crime ties, and some CIA documents remained classified for decades — these are real, documented facts worth taking seriously.

The leap happens when those loose threads get read as a single hidden design. Gaps in the record, witnesses who disagreed, a bullet trajectory that required explanation — each ambiguity gets assigned to a coordinating intelligence. But ambiguity is exactly what you'd expect from a shocking, fast-moving event investigated under political pressure in 1963. The fear that powerful institutions would murder a president and bury the truth is not irrational on its face, and that fear makes suggestive details feel like confirmation. Notice, though, that the theory only holds if you accept a machine so vast — spanning the CIA, Dallas police, the Warren Commission, Ruby, and dozens of witnesses — that any missing evidence simply proves how good the cover-up was. When no finding can count against a claim, the claim is doing something other than reasoning from evidence. The simpler read: a troubled, attention-seeking gunman, an imperfect investigation, and a grieving nation hungry for an explanation equal to its loss. Which of those actually requires more proof?
