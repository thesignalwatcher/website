Explanation of Belief

The theory asserts that schools are officially adopting policies to accommodate students who identify as non-human animals (otherkin or therians), including providing litter boxes and allowing animal-like behavior in classrooms. It further contends that acceptance of transgender students logically compels schools to extend identical accommodations to students claiming non-human identities, framing the otherkin phenomenon as evidence that gender identity policies are inherently absurd.

Origins: The claim circulated widely on conservative social media platforms and political commentary spaces beginning around 2021–2022, often amplified by figures such as Matt Walsh and various right-wing commentators, gaining traction alongside broader culture-war debates about school gender policies. No verified instance of a school formally adopting an otherkin accommodation policy or installing litter boxes for student use has been confirmed by journalists or school officials who investigated the claims.

How believers defend it: Adherents typically treat official school denials and journalistic debunking as evidence of institutional cover-up or media complicity, arguing that administrators would not publicly admit to such policies. Some proponents shift the argument to anecdotal accounts or viral social media posts as sufficient proof, contending that mainstream fact-checkers are ideologically motivated to suppress the story.

Voice of Reason

There's a real subculture here worth acknowledging: some teenagers do identify under labels like 'therian' or 'otherkin,' often rooted in online communities, creative expression, or a genuine sense of personal identity. That's not invented. What is invented — repeatedly, and without a single verified case — is the claim that schools have adopted formal policies accommodating non-human identities, or that litter boxes have been placed in classrooms for students. Journalists and school districts across the country have looked directly at these specific rumors and found no supporting evidence: no policy documents, no administrative memos, no corroborating accounts.

The reasoning jumps from 'this subculture exists online' to 'schools must be officially enabling it' — a gap filled by assumption, not documentation. The claim works emotionally because it packages a genuine cultural anxiety about identity and institutions into a vivid, almost absurd image. Once you've accepted that schools are secretly captured by ideology, ambiguous anecdotes start to feel like proof. But the litter-box story in particular spread widely before anyone asked the basic question: where, exactly? No answer has ever come. The simpler read is that a real online subculture got weaponized into a policy panic. Worth asking what evidence would actually change your mind either way.
