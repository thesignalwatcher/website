Voice of Reason

Adrenochrome is a real chemical — a byproduct of adrenaline oxidation, studied in early psychiatric research. It is also commercially synthesized and widely available for laboratory use. That is the entire kernel here: a real word, a real compound. What the claim does with it is a leap: it strips away everything documented and replaces it with properties the compound simply does not have — extreme psychoactivity, anti-aging effects, a demand so enormous it requires industrial child trafficking. No verified pharmacology or clinical record supports those properties.

The claim then adds a layer that makes it unfalsifiable: a hidden machine so vast and so powerful that its absence from any evidence trail is itself taken as proof of the cover-up. Once that assumption is accepted, every ambiguous detail — a celebrity who looks young, an unexplained news story — feels like confirmation. That feeling is real, but it is the experience of a pattern seeming to click into place, not evidence that the pattern exists. The claim also revives a centuries-old accusation, targeting Jewish people, that has a documented history of inciting violence. That history is worth sitting with before lending the idea credibility.

Thompson's *Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas* (1971). The conspiracy appears to have reframed these literary and folkloric elements into a literal, political allegation.

Propagation followed a recognizable pattern for QAnon-adjacent content: initial circulation through Q drops and image-board threads, then amplification via YouTube and Telegram channels, and eventually wider reach through meme formats purporting to show celebrity "evidence." The claim reached mainstream attention during QAnon's notable surge in 2020, a period reflected in documented Google Trends spikes for the term.
